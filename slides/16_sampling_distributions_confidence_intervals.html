SAP’s profitability claim

SAP advertised that its customers were 32% more profitable than their peers.

Nucleus Research examined 81 publicly traded customers listed on SAP’s website.

Their measure was return on equity (ROE): annual profit as a percentage of shareholder equity.

The SAP advertisements ran in 2005; Nucleus published Research Note G12 in March 2006. The source is linked at first use. Each row of sap_roe represents one company, with its ROE and industry-average ROE in percent. The course notes’ “SAP dispute” section supplies the case context and further sources. Is 81 too small?

SAP objected: 81 firms out of roughly 30,000 customers is only 0.27%.

What determines how much the sample average varies?

Ask students what information they need before judging precision. Do not settle the case from the sampling fraction. Collect suggestions about company variability, sample size, and selection. Return to selection at the end.

A random-sampling model

Model the 81 company ROEs as iid draws from the customer population.

Each customer is equally likely on each draw.

Website selection may violate this model.

This is a working model, not a claim that website selection was random. Sampling with replacement gives iid draws exactly. For simple random sampling without replacement, the SE correction is sqrt((N-n)/(N-1)), about 0.9987 for n=81 and N=30,000. Thus the iid SE is a close approximation here. Avoid the different claim that the chance of any repeated firm is negligible.

Parameter, estimator, and estimate

Quantity SAP example
Parameter \(\mu\): mean ROE of all SAP customers.
Estimator \(\bar X\): the rule for averaging a random sample.
Estimate \(\bar x=12.6\%\): the result from these 81 firms.

Which would change with a new sample?

The parameter and the averaging rule stay fixed; the realized estimate usually changes. The estimator is a random variable before the sample is observed. The observed estimate is one realized value. An average here plays the same mathematical role as average project profit in Thaler, but mu is unknown.

The sample mean’s center and spread

For iid draws with mean \(\mu\) and finite variance \(\sigma^2\),

\[ E[\bar X]=\mu,\qquad \operatorname{SD}(\bar X)=\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt n}. \]

These are the same rules we used for average project profit.

Standard error

The standard error is the SD of an estimator’s sampling distribution.

\[ SE(\bar X)=\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt n}. \]

For the sample mean under iid sampling, it measures the typical size of estimation error.

Estimate SE from the sample

The population SD \(\sigma\) is unknown. Use the sample SD \(s\):

\[ \widehat{SE}(\bar X)=\frac{s}{\sqrt n}. \]

This estimates how much sample means vary across repeated samples.

The hat marks an estimated SE. In the app sigma is known because we created the population. In an actual analysis we substitute s; it changes across samples. The same units apply to the estimate, its error, and its SE.

A normal approximation for the mean

\[ \bar X\approx N\!\left(\mu,\frac{\sigma^2}{n}\right). \]

The curve uses the simulated population’s known mean and variance. The CLT supplies the approximate shape; the center and variance follow exactly from the iid model. The second normal parameter is variance. Whether n is large enough depends on the population’s shape and extreme outcomes.

Estimation error

Subtract the fixed population mean:

\[ \bar X-\mu\approx N\!\left(0,\frac{\sigma^2}{n}\right). \]

The errors are centered at zero and have SD \(\sigma/\sqrt n\).

We cannot calculate our actual error because \(\mu\) is unknown.

In about 95% of random samples, the sample mean is within two SEs of \(\mu\)

This uses the normal approximation for estimation error.

The exact standard-normal area between -2 and 2 is about 0.9545. We use the course’s approximate two-SE rule throughout; do not call it exact 95% coverage.

Turn the distance into an interval

\[ |\bar X-\mu|\leq 2\,SE \]

is the same event as

\[ \bar X-2\,SE\leq\mu\leq\bar X+2\,SE. \]

Centering the interval on \(\bar X\) captures \(\mu\) in about 95% of samples.

A confidence interval for the mean

A confidence interval gives plausible values for the population mean.

With an adequate normal approximation and an estimated SE, use

\[ \bar x\ \pm\ 2\frac{s}{\sqrt n}. \]

This is an approximate 95% confidence interval under the sampling model.

Mean 42 minutes

Assume the normal approximation is adequate.

A true mean above 45 minutes would trigger a costly process review.

Find the estimated SE and an approximate 95% interval for the population mean.

Plausible mean delivery times

\[ \widehat{SE}=\frac{12}{\sqrt{100}}=1.2\text{ minutes}, \]

\[ 42\pm 2(1.2)=[39.6,\ 44.4]\text{ minutes}. \]

Does this audit support triggering that review?

The upper endpoint, 44.4 minutes, is below 45. Under the sampling assumptions, the audit does not support triggering a review on the grounds that the mean exceeds 45. Have pairs state this in one sentence. The interval gives plausible values for the population mean; it does not contain 95% of individual times. Individual spread remains much larger.

Plan the next audit

Use 12 minutes as the planning value for the population SD.

How many deliveries would give a two-SE margin of error of 1 minute?

\[ 2\frac{12}{\sqrt n}=1 \]

Solving gives \(\sqrt n=24\), so the audit needs 576 deliveries.

Allow two minutes before revealing the answer. Planning uses the previous sample SD as a stand-in for the unknown population SD. At least 576 deliveries targets a margin no larger than 1 minute if that planning value remains adequate.

The 95% describes the procedure

About 95% of intervals from this procedure contain \(\mu\) under the model.

The population mean stays fixed; the samples and intervals change.

Our one interval either contains \(\mu\) or misses it.

Ask students whether the procedure assigns a 0.95 probability to mu being in the particular interval already calculated. It does not: the frequentist coverage statement concerns repetition of the sampling procedure. In real analyses the sample SD also changes, so interval widths can vary.

SAP’s interval

\[ \widehat{SE}=\frac{25.66}{\sqrt{81}} \approx 2.85\text{ percentage points}. \]

\[ \bar x\pm2\widehat{SE}\approx [6.9\%,\ 18.3\%]. \]

These are plausible population means under the random-sampling model.

Compare the claims with the interval

Treating the industry benchmark as fixed, which claims fit this interval?

A mean near 7% would be much worse than Nucleus claimed, and it also fits.

The industry benchmark 15.7% lies inside the interval. Both lower and higher population means are plausible; the data do not establish that SAP customers are less profitable. The lower end near 7% permits substantial underperformance, so including parity does not establish that the firms perform well either. Applying a 32% advantage to that benchmark gives about 20.7%, outside the interval. These readings all depend on the working model and the fixed-benchmark simplification; do not make an unconditional claim about the advertisement or about SAP causing profit changes.

How were the firms selected?

Nucleus used publicly traded firms listed on SAP’s website.

They may differ from SAP’s other customers.

The interval measures sampling variation under the model. It does not correct selection bias.

Report what the data support

Speak for Nucleus, SAP, or an independent analyst.

Use the interval and identify one limitation.

Then write a one-sentence conclusion about mean customer ROE.

Have tables discuss for two minutes, then hear one response per role. Distinguish the sample-size objection from representativeness. The fraction 81/30,000 alone does not establish that a sample is useless. The comparison does not identify SAP’s causal effect on profitability. A defensible conclusion reports 6.9% to 18.3% under random sampling, with the selection limitation attached.
