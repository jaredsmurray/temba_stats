TEMBA Statistics 2026

Sale prices and living area

Each point is one of 438 homes sold in a single ZIP code in southeast Austin.

A 2,000 sq ft home comes on the market. What price would you predict?

These are different homes from the three ZIP codes in Chapter 13, but size and price are about as closely related: r = 0.73 here versus 0.77 there. Take a few guesses. Most people look at the homes near 2,000 sq ft and average them by eye. The line does the same thing in a disciplined way. We come back to this listing in Use 2.

A fitted line

The line runs through the bulk of the data:

\[ \begin{aligned} \widehat{\text{price}} = {} & 100{,}771 \\ & + 100.01 \times \text{area} \end{aligned} \]

We estimated its intercept and slope from these 438 homes.

Hats mark estimates

\[ \hat y = \hat\beta_0 + \hat\beta_1 x \]

  • \(\hat\beta_0 = 100{,}771\) estimates the true intercept \(\beta_0\), and \(\hat\beta_1 = 100.01\) estimates the true slope \(\beta_1\).
  • A hat marks a number computed from data, just like \(\hat p\) for a sample proportion.
  • \(\hat y\) is a predicted value: the height of the line above \(x\).

Greek letters are unknown parameters, as in Chapter 8. We’ll write the model that defines beta_0 and beta_1 in a few slides.

Fitted values and residuals

A residual is the observed price minus the fitted value on the line. A positive residual means the home sold for more than the line predicts for its size.

The smaller home sold for less than the larger one, but it did better relative to its size. Residuals carry information about everything besides living area: location on the block, condition, age, and so on.

Least squares minimizes the residual variance

Among all possible lines, least squares chooses the one whose residuals have the smallest variance.

All three lines pass through the point of averages, so each set of residuals averages zero and the residual variance is the average squared residual. The orange segments are residuals for 70 of the homes. Tilting the line either way makes the typical residual bigger. We won’t derive the minimizing line; software finds it. The criterion is in the notes.

The slope is correlation times a ratio of SDs

\[ \hat\beta_1 = r\,\frac{s_y}{s_x} = 0.73 \times \frac{72{,}263}{527} \approx 100 \text{ dollars per sq ft} \]

  • Here \(r\) is the correlation from Chapter 13, and \(s_x\) and \(s_y\) are the standard deviations of area and price.
  • A home one SD larger than average (527 sq ft) has a predicted price \(r = 0.73\) SDs above average.
  • Two relationships with the same correlation can have different slopes when their outcomes vary by different amounts.

0.73 SDs of price is about $52,800. Hang onto the last bullet: it explains the Tesla and Walmart comparison at the end.

Residuals in sales forecasting

A retailer predicts monthly sales from advertising spend, both in thousands of dollars.

\[ \widehat{\text{sales}} = 125 + 3.2 \times \text{advertising} \]

Month Advertising Sales

  1. Find the fitted value and residual for each month.
  2. Which month did better relative to what its advertising predicts?
  3. The CFO is worried about April’s negative residual. Was April a bad month?

Source: exercise ex-slr-sales-residuals in the notes. Allow three or four minutes.

Residuals in sales forecasting: solution

Month Fitted value Residual
March \(125 + 3.2 \times 40 = 253\) \(265 - 253 = +12\)
April \(125 + 3.2 \times 50 = 285\) \(275 - 285 = -10\)

April had higher sales, but March beat its prediction by $12,000 while April fell $10,000 short.

A negative residual means April sold less than the line predicts for $50,000 of advertising, not that April was a bad month. Residuals average zero, so some months always land below the line.

April’s shortfall could be ordinary month-to-month variation, seasonality, or anything else the model leaves out.

The regression model

\[ \begin{gathered} y = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x + \epsilon \\ \epsilon \sim N(0, \sigma^2) \end{gathered} \]

  • The line gives the average price of all homes of each size.
  • Prices of homes the same size are normally distributed around the line.
  • The standard deviation \(\sigma\) is the same at every size.

The gray point is one 2,800 sq ft home, and epsilon is its distance from the true line. We drew the picture using our estimates as if they were the truth. In practice we never see the true line.

Estimates stand in for unknown parameters

True value (unknown) Estimate
Intercept \(\beta_0\) \(\hat\beta_0 = 100{,}771\)
Slope \(\beta_1\) \(\hat\beta_1 = 100.01\)
SD around the line \(\sigma\) \(s_e = 49{,}443\)

The residual standard error, \(s_e\), is essentially the SD of the residuals.

Another sample of homes would give different estimates. Standard errors measure how far estimates tend to fall from the truth, as they did for \(\bar x\) and \(\hat p\).

Use 1: Summarizing trends

The slope compares average prices

Comparing homes that differ by 1,000 sq ft, the larger ones sold for about $100,000 more on average.

Three equivalent readings, from the notes: the line rises $100.01 per square foot; two homes a square foot apart have predicted prices $100.01 apart; and, across many pairs of homes a square foot apart, the larger ones sell for $100.01 more on average. The last one is the model’s reading: each point on the line is the average price of all homes of that size.

The intercept is an extrapolation here

The smallest home in the data has 852 sq ft, so the intercept is an extrapolation to zero square feet with no practical meaning.

Someone usually proposes an empty lot as a zero-square-foot house. Even granting that, zero is far outside the data, and nothing says the line holds down there.

An intercept with a meaning

Course evaluations for 463 UT Austin sections (Hamermesh and Parker, 2005).

Each instructor’s beauty rating is shifted so that 0 is the average rating.

The intercept, 4.00, is the predicted evaluation for an instructor of average beauty.

Six students rated each instructor’s photo. Because beauty has mean zero and the line passes through the point of averages, the intercept is also the average evaluation. Centering area the same way would make the housing intercept the predicted price of an average-sized home; the slope would not change.

Plausible values for the slope

\[ \begin{aligned} &\hat\beta_1 \pm 2\,\text{SE}(\hat\beta_1) \\ &= 100.01 \pm 2(4.48) \\ &\approx [91,\ 109] \end{aligned} \]

Plausible values for the true slope run from about $91 to $109 per square foot.

A flat line, with slope zero, is far outside that range.

The regression table’s interval, [91.20, 108.83], differs only in the decimals. The dashed lines pivot around the point of averages; they show the range of slopes the data support, not a full band for the line.

Testing for a linear relationship

A true slope of zero means no linear relationship: the line is flat.

\[ H_0: \beta_1 = 0 \qquad H_A: \beta_1 \neq 0 \]

\[ t = \frac{\hat\beta_1 - 0}{\text{SE}(\hat\beta_1)} = \frac{100.01}{4.48} \approx 22.3 \]

The estimate is about 22 standard errors from zero, so the p-value is far below 0.001. These are the \(t\) and p-value in the slope row of the regression table.

The test agrees with the interval: zero is not a plausible slope. The intercept row has its own test of beta_0 = 0, which is of no practical interest here for the reasons on the intercept slide.

Use 2: Making predictions

Predicting a 2,000 sq ft home

\[ \widehat{\text{price}} = 100{,}771 + 100.01 \times 2{,}000 \approx \$300{,}791 \]

A rough 95% prediction interval

\[ \widehat{\text{price}} \pm 2 s_e = 300{,}791 \pm 2 \times 49{,}443 \approx [\$201{,}905,\ \$399{,}677] \]

The model says about 95% of 2,000 sq ft homes sell within \(2 s_e\) of the line.

In our data, 93% of the homes fall between the dashed lines. Software computes a slightly more careful interval that also accounts for uncertainty in the line; here it is almost identical.

Three kinds of typical error

Typical distance Housing
Standard deviation, \(s_y\) from prices to their average $72,263
Standard error, \(\text{SE}(\hat\beta_1)\) from \(\hat\beta_1\) to the true slope $4.48
Residual standard error, \(s_e\) from prices to the line $49,443

Which of these would shrink if we had four times as many homes?

The standard error would be about half as large, because the estimate improves with more data. \(s_y\) and \(s_e\) would stay about the same, because sale prices would be no less variable.

Take answers before revealing. This distinction drives most of the confusion about regression output: a tiny SE for the slope says the line is well estimated, not that predictions for individual homes are precise.

Extrapolation

We fit the line to homes from 852 to 3,319 sq ft. These data tell us nothing about how prices behave for a 10,000 sq ft home.

Price per square foot for luxury properties can look quite different, and they sit in different neighborhoods. The intercept slide was the same problem at the other end of the line.

Use 3: Measuring strength

Predicting with and without living area

The colored homes are the same in both panels. Which set of prediction errors is smaller?

We’ll call the left panel the null model: it forces the slope to zero, matching the null hypothesis beta_1 = 0.

Prediction errors with and without living area

Using living area shrinks the typical prediction error from $72,263 to $49,443.

Each home’s price splits into two parts

\[{\color{#0072B2}y} = {\color{#009E73}\hat y} + {\color{#D55E00}e}\]

The two homes from the residuals slide. Each price is the line’s prediction for a home that size plus a residual: the part of the price that size alone doesn’t predict.

Variance splits the same way

\(\text{Var}({\color{#0072B2}y}) = \text{Var}({\color{#009E73}\hat y}) + \text{Var}({\color{#D55E00}e})\)

In thousands of dollars:

\[{\color{#0072B2}72.3^2} \approx {\color{#009E73}52.8^2} + {\color{#D55E00}49.4^2}\] \[ \begin{aligned} R^2 &= ({\color{#009E73}52.8} / {\color{#0072B2}72.3})^2 \\ &= 1 - ({\color{#D55E00}49.4} / {\color{#0072B2}72.3})^2 \\ &= 0.53 \end{aligned} \]

Standard deviations don’t add; variances do, because the fitted values and the residuals are uncorrelated. R-squared is the share of the variance in prices that the line predicts from size.

\(R^2\) measures the reduction in prediction variance

  • Using living area cuts the variance of our prediction errors by about 53%.
  • \(R^2\) is also the squared correlation: \(0.73^2 \approx 0.53\).
  • An \(R^2\) of 0 means the line predicts no better than the average; an \(R^2\) of 1 means every point sits on the line.

“Explained” in “proportion of variance explained” really means “predicted.” Predicting size from price gives exactly the same R-squared.

A low \(R^2\) does not mean no relationship

\(R^2 = 0.036\): beauty predicts about 4% of the variation in evaluations.

The 95% CI for the slope is [0.07, 0.20], and \(t = 4.1\).

The data give strong evidence of a weak linear relationship.

Knowing beauty barely changes the typical error: 0.55 points without it and 0.55 with it. Clarity and fair grading almost surely matter more, and they end up in the residuals. Whether there is a linear relationship is a question about the slope, not about R-squared.

\(R^2\) and the size of prediction errors

A logistics company predicts weekly delivery costs from the number of shipments. The model has \(R^2 = 0.77\) and \(s_e = \$3{,}200\). Weekly costs have a standard deviation of \(s_y = \$6{,}700\).

The CFO says, “An \(R^2\) of 0.77 seems low. I don’t want forecasts that miss by more than $7,000 very often.”

  1. How much smaller is the typical prediction error using shipments than predicting the average cost every week?
  2. Is the model precise enough for the CFO?

Source: exercise ex-slr-rsquared-interpretation in the notes. Allow three minutes.

\(R^2\) and the size of prediction errors: solution

Typical prediction error
Predicting the average cost $6,700
Using shipments $3,200
Reduction $3,500

A $7,000 miss is \(7{,}000 / 3{,}200 \approx 2.2\) residual standard errors. Misses that large should happen in fewer than 5% of weeks, so the model meets the CFO’s standard.

\(R^2\) is a relative measure. To judge the size of errors in dollars, look at \(s_e\).

Case study: The market model

The market model: \(R = \alpha + \beta R_M + \epsilon\)

Beta is the slope: a stock’s average move per 1% move in the market. Alpha is the intercept: its average return when the market is flat.

Alpha and beta are finance’s names for beta_0 and beta_1. R is the stock’s return, R_M the market’s, and epsilon the stock-specific part of the return. Beta above 1 amplifies market swings in both directions; below 1 dampens them.

Tesla and Ford

Each point is one week of returns, October 2023 to September 2025; the market is the S&P 500 (SPY). The dashed line has slope 1.

Which stock amplifies market moves? Roughly what is each beta?

Pause here and take guesses before moving on.

Returns in percent. When the market moves 1%, Tesla moves about 1.87% on average. The intercept, \(\hat\alpha\), is small next to its standard error.

The \(t\) of 5.60 tests \(\beta = 0\). For a stock, the more useful question is whether \(\beta = 1\).

Nobody doubts that Tesla moves with the market. The question an investor cares about is whether it moves more than one-for-one.

The 95% intervals for alpha are about [-1.46, 1.43] for Tesla and [-1.06, 0.58] for Ford, percent per week.

Is Tesla’s beta different from 1?

Stock \(\hat\beta\) \(\hat\beta \pm 2\,\text{SE}\) Is 1 plausible?
Tesla 1.87 [1.20, 2.54] No
Ford 0.98 [0.60, 1.36] Yes

\[ t = \frac{\hat\beta - 1}{\text{SE}(\hat\beta)} = \frac{1.873 - 1}{0.335} \approx 2.61, \qquad \text{p-value} \approx 0.009 \]

We have good evidence that Tesla amplifies market moves. Ford’s estimate is just under 1, and the data are consistent with a beta of 1.

Similar \(R^2\), different betas

Both correlations with the market are about 0.48.

This is the payoff of the slope formula from the start of the lecture: same r, different s_y, different slope. R-squared is a relative measure, so the two stocks can share it while Tesla’s residuals are far larger: s_e = 7.19% versus 2.59%.

Each week’s return splits into two parts

\[{\color{#0072B2}y} = {\color{#009E73}\hat y} + {\color{#D55E00}e}\]

Two weeks of Tesla returns. The week ending April 26, 2024, the market rose 2.6%; the line predicted 4.9% for Tesla and it returned 14.4%, a residual of 9.5%. The week ending October 20, 2023, the market fell 2.4%; the line predicted a 4.5% loss and Tesla lost 15.6%. Every week splits this way: a market part on the line, plus a stock-specific part off it.

Variance splits the same way

\(\text{Var}({\color{#0072B2}y}) = \text{Var}({\color{#009E73}\hat y}) + \text{Var}({\color{#D55E00}e})\)

\[{\color{#0072B2}8.17^2} \approx {\color{#009E73}3.96^2} + {\color{#D55E00}7.15^2}\] \[ \begin{aligned} R^2 &= ({\color{#009E73}3.96} / {\color{#0072B2}8.17})^2 \\ &= 1 - ({\color{#D55E00}7.15} / {\color{#0072B2}8.17})^2 \\ &= 0.23 \end{aligned} \]

\[{\color{#0072B2}2.94^2} \approx {\color{#009E73}1.40^2} + {\color{#D55E00}2.58^2}\] \[ \begin{aligned} R^2 &= ({\color{#009E73}1.40} / {\color{#0072B2}2.94})^2 \\ &= 1 - ({\color{#D55E00}2.58} / {\color{#0072B2}2.94})^2 \\ &= 0.23 \end{aligned} \]

Standard deviations don’t add; variances do, because the market part and the residual are uncorrelated. Beta sets how much market risk a stock carries: Tesla’s market part is almost three times as wide as Walmart’s. R-squared is the market’s share of the total, and it is the same for both. Diversification can only shrink the orange part.

Similar betas, different \(R^2\)

Apple and Ford both move about one-for-one with the market on average. Ford’s returns scatter more around its line, so the market predicts less of their variation.

Beta tells us whether a stock amplifies or dampens market moves. R-squared tells us what share of its week-to-week variation the market predicts.

Abnormal returns are residuals

A residual is Tesla’s return minus what the market model predicts for that week. Finance calls it an abnormal return. The dashed lines are two SDs above and below zero.

With no news at all we’d expect about 5% of weeks, roughly 5 of 104, outside the dashed lines; 6 are. The largest, the week of October 24, 2024, followed a strong third-quarter earnings report. Others line up with the July 2024 delivery numbers, the June 2025 Musk-Trump spat, the January 2024 growth warning, the October 2024 robotaxi event, and the rally after the 2024 election. Sources are linked in the notes. Not every extreme residual has a tidy explanation, and news rarely accounts for all of one.
