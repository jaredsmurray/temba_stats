16  Sampling Distributions and Confidence Intervals

16.1 The SAP dispute

In 2005 SAP, the world’s largest maker of enterprise software, ran advertisements claiming that companies running SAP are 32% more profitable than companies that don’t. The figure came from a study SAP commissioned and never released for public scrutiny (Carr 2005, 2006). In March 2006 the analyst firm Nucleus Research published a rebuttal (Nucleus Research 2006). Nucleus collected data on the 81 publicly traded companies listed as customers on SAP’s website. For each company, it looked up return on equity (ROE), annual profit as a percentage of shareholder equity, along with the average ROE of the company’s industry. ROE measures how profitably a company uses its shareholders’ capital.

The 81 SAP customers averaged an ROE of 12.6% against industry benchmarks averaging 15.7%, and Nucleus titled the report “SAP customers are 20 percent less profitable than their peers” (12.6 is about 20% below 15.7).

SAP dismissed the report as “junk science.” Its argument was about sample size: 81 companies out of SAP’s roughly 30,000 customers is about a quarter of one percent, far too few — SAP claimed — to say anything about the customer base as a whole. Who is right? Both sides are arguing about the same unknown quantity, the average ROE among all 30,000 customers. The next few chapters build the tools to say what 81 companies can and cannot tell us about it.

The sap_roe data contain the 81 companies from the Nucleus report, one row per company: its name (company), its industry (industry), its ROE in percent (roe), and the average ROE of its industry (industry_roe). Figure 16.1 shows the 81 ROE values along with the two averages in dispute.

Company ROEs run from -91.8% to 116.4%, with a standard deviation of 25.7 percentage points. Because the sample mean is sensitive to extreme values, including or excluding a few firms could change it appreciably. How much would the mean vary across random samples of 81 companies?

To reason about an error we can’t observe, we need a model for how the sample arose. For now, consider the 81 companies as a simple random sample of SAP customers: a lottery draw in which every group of 81 customers is equally likely to be selected. Since 81 is a small fraction of the population, we can approximate this sampling process with iid random variables \(X_1, \dots, X_{81}\), drawn from a discrete distribution placing probability \(1/N\) on each of the \(N\) companies in the population. This is the same thought exercise we went through in the introductory probability chapter. The true population mean and standard deviation of that distribution are \(\mu\) and \(\sigma\), respectively — the mean and SD we would get if we could see all 30,000 firms’ ROE.

Technical detail 16.1: Approximating a random sample with iid draws

If the connection between sampling firms and drawing random variables still feels abstract, the table below may help. Table 16.1 shows the probability distribution of a single firm drawn completely at random from the population.

Let \(X\) be the ROE of a company drawn at random, with every company equally likely. If company \(i\) has ROE \(x_i\), the mean of this distribution is

\[ \mu = \frac{1}{30{,}000}\sum_{i=1}^{30{,}000}x_i = \frac{x_1 + x_2 + \cdots + x_{30{,}000}}{30{,}000}. \]

This is the average ROE over the whole population. The population variance \(\sigma^2\) and standard deviation \(\sigma\) are computed similarly, using the discrete variance and standard deviation formulas. Just as before, we can interpret \(\mu\) as the expected value for the ROE of a single firm sampled from this population and \(\sigma\) as a measure of how far that firm’s ROE is likely to be from \(\mu\).

Our thought experiment treats the observed sample of 81 firms as iid observations, like a series of coin tosses or dice rolls. However, when working with surveys of a finite population, a random sample typically selects firms without replacement — no firm could appear more than once — producing dependence between the observations (if firm A appears, firm B is less likely to appear because one “slot” of the 81 is missing). However: a) the dependence is very weak when the sample size is much smaller than the population, and b) ignoring this particular kind of dependence actually produces a conservative estimate of uncertainty.

Before we continue, we need to collect a few definitions and notation conventions.

Definition 16.1: Parameter, estimator, and estimate

  • The parameter is a fixed numerical property of the population or probability distribution that we want to estimate — here the true average ROE \(\mu\) among all of SAP’s customers.

  • An estimator for a parameter is the recipe (a mathematical formula) applied to a random sample — for example, the sample mean \(\bar{X}\) is the arithmetic average we defined in the definition of the sample mean, computed on a random sample. It’s a random variable because the sample over which we take the average is random.

  • The estimate is the number the recipe produced from our actual sample: in the SAP example, \(\bar{x} = 12.6\%\).

  • The estimation error \(\bar{X} - \mu\) is random; it depends on the sample we observe. Since we can describe the probability distribution of \(\bar X\), we can describe the probability distribution of our estimation error.

  • The realized estimation error \(\bar{x} - \mu\) is a single fixed number we cannot calculate from the sample alone, because it depends on the unknown \(\mu\).

The essential question in this dispute is this: Does the estimate of 12.6% ROE among the 81 firms provide strong evidence that SAP firms have below-average (15.7%) ROE?

16.2 Sampling distributions and confidence intervals

Suppose thousands of analysts each drew a random sample of 81 SAP customers and computed its mean ROE. Their estimates would vary around the population mean \(\mu\). If that variation were small, a single sample mean would usually be close to \(\mu\); a wide spread would allow larger estimation errors. We describe this variation with the sampling distribution.

Definition 16.2: Sampling distribution

The sampling distribution of a statistic (like the sample mean) is the probability distribution of that statistic across random samples of a given size \(n\). For the sample mean, it is the distribution of \(\bar{X}\) — the values our recipe could have produced, had the sample come out differently.

In real life we get one sample, so we never see the sampling distribution directly.

Under the iid model, we know two things about this distribution exactly. The rules for sums and averages of iid random variables give \(E[\bar X] = \mu\) and \(SD(\bar X) = \sigma/\sqrt{n}\). The Central Limit Theorem (CLT) adds the shape: the sampling distribution of the sample mean is approximately normal when the iid model has finite variance and the sample size is large enough:

\[ \bar{X} \;\approx\; N\!\left(\mu,\; \sigma^2/n\right). \]

In words: across random samples, the sample mean is right on average, and its typical distance from the truth shrinks as the sample grows.

Using our rules for shifting and scaling random variables, which apply to normal distributions too, we can get the distribution of the estimation error \(\bar{X} - \mu\) over many random samples by shifting the mean to zero:

\[ \bar{X} - \mu \;\approx\; N\!\left(0,\; \sigma^2/n\right). \]

Across random samples, the errors average to zero. Their standard deviation, \(\sigma/\sqrt{n}\), describes how far individual sample means typically fall from \(\mu\); we call it the standard error of the sample mean.

Definition 16.3: Standard error

The standard error of a statistic is the standard deviation of its sampling distribution. For a statistic that is right on average, like the sample mean, it is the typical size of the estimation error:

\[ SE(\bar{X}) = \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}} \approx \frac{s}{\sqrt{n}}, \]

and the number we report is an estimate, computed by plugging in the sample standard deviation \(s\) for the unknown \(\sigma\).

For the SAP sample the ingredients are \(s = 25.7\) and \(n = 81\), so

\[ SE(\bar{X}) \approx \frac{s}{\sqrt{81}} \approx 2.85 \text{ percentage points}. \]

In words: when we estimate the average ROE of all SAP customers from 81 of them, our estimate typically misses by about 2.9 percentage points. The gap Nucleus built its headline on is 3.1 points — almost exactly the size of a typical error induced by using a random sample of size 81 instead of the entire population.

16.2.2 Accounting for sampling error when presenting results

Let’s return to SAP’s objection: They said, essentially, that a sample of 81 firms was far too small to learn anything about 30,000 firms. We know that is, at best, incomplete — if the population variability of ROE was very low, we could learn an awful lot from the average of 81 observations. A more charitable read of the objection would be: Given the variability in ROE across the population of firms, if the true average ROE of SAP firms were 15.7% (the industry average), then an observed difference of \(12.6 - 15.7 = -3.1\) percentage points between the sample estimate and true value is perfectly consistent with chance variability due to random sampling. Concluding that SAP firms have below-average profitability would be a mistake. (SAP is actually making a stronger statement, that their firms have average ROE 32% higher than average, which would be about 20.7%.)

We can avoid making a guess about the true population mean entirely if we ask a different, related question: What values for the true population average are consistent with the data we observed? The sample average ROE is our best guess for the population average ROE, but we know that it is off by some amount due to the random sampling process. What we would like to do is report an interval: Our best guess plus or minus a margin of error that describes the range of true values we can’t rule out with a high degree of certainty given the natural variability in ROE — measured by \(\sigma\), estimated by \(s\) — and the sample size \(n\).

We already established that \(\bar X - \mu\), our error, has an approximately normal distribution with mean zero and standard deviation \(SE(\bar X) = \sigma/\sqrt{n}\). That means the interval \[ \bar X \pm 2\underbrace{SE(\bar X)}_{ = \sigma/\sqrt{n}} \] will contain \(\mu\) in about 95% of all possible random samples of size \(n\). Why? Since \(\bar X-\mu \approx N(0, SE(\bar X)^2)\), \[ \Pr[-2SE(\bar X) \leq \bar X - \mu \leq 2 SE(\bar X)] \approx 0.95. \] A little algebra turns this into \[ \Pr[\bar X - 2SE(\bar X) \leq \mu \leq \bar X + 2 SE(\bar X)] \approx 0.95. \]

We can plug in the observed estimate \(\bar x\) and \(s/\sqrt{n}\), the estimate of \(SE(\bar X)\) computed in the definition of standard error, to get a computable interval:

\[ \bar x \pm 2\frac{s}{\sqrt{n}} \]

We call an interval constructed this way a confidence interval, and it represents all possible values for the true population mean that are consistent with the data we have, at a given level of confidence (95% here).

Definition 16.4: Confidence interval

A confidence interval for a parameter is a range of plausible values for that parameter (at a given confidence level, usually 95%). These are values for the true parameter that are consistent with the data we observed; the difference between a plausible value and our best guess (\(\bar x\)) can be reasonably explained by chance variation due to sampling.

For an estimator whose sampling distribution is approximately normal and centered at the parameter, an approximate 95% confidence interval is

\[ \text{estimate} \pm 2 \times \text{(standard error)}. \]

The 95% describes repeated use of the procedure: across many random samples of the same size, about 95% of the intervals contain the fixed population parameter. For the sample mean, we estimate the standard error with \(s/\sqrt n\), so the interval’s width also varies from sample to sample. Its coverage is approximate.

For the SAP sample,

\[ 12.6 \pm 2(2.85) \approx [6.9\%,\ 18.3\%]. \]

TipInterpretation: what the interval says about the dispute

The plausible values for the true average ROE among all SAP customers run from about 6.9% to about 18.3%. Parity with peers is one of them: the 15.7% industry benchmark sits inside the interval, so the 3.1-point gap Nucleus led with is consistent with ordinary sampling variation. So is an average ROE near 7%, which would be considerably worse than Nucleus claimed. Using the industry benchmark for this comparison, SAP’s advertised 32% advantage implies about 20.7%, which lies outside the interval and is not a plausible value at this confidence level.

We can get a feel for what a confidence interval represents and how it behaves in the app. Tick “Show approximate 95% confidence interval” and every sample now draws its interval along the bottom of the sampling distribution, colored green when it covers the dashed line at \(\mu\) and red when it misses.

As you keep sampling, the footer keeps a running tally of how often the interval covered \(\mu\). The tally bounces around early on — after 50 samples ours read 90.0% — and settles near 95% as the samples pile up. Figure 16.8 draws the first 100 intervals separately instead of one at a time, so you can see each miss the tracker counted.

WarningCommon mistake: The 95% describes the procedure, not this interval

\(\mu\) is a fixed number, and \([6.9\%, 18.3\%]\) either contains it or it doesn’t — there is no randomness left to attach a probability to.

The 95% statement belongs to the method: intervals built this way contain \(\mu\) in about 95% of random samples. Over many independent data analyses for this class where the sampling assumptions hold, about 95% of the intervals will contain the true value and about 5% will miss. We can’t know which intervals miss, but we know it doesn’t happen too often.

16.3 Practice

Exercise 16.1: What is being estimated?

A retailer wants the mean delivery time \(\mu\) for all orders in its current service area. In an invented study, it takes a random sample of 100 orders and computes an average delivery time of 42 minutes.

Identify the parameter, the estimator, and the estimate. If the retailer drew a new random sample, which of these would change and which would stay fixed?

Solution

The parameter is the population mean delivery time \(\mu\). The estimator is the sample mean \(\bar{X}\), the rule for averaging the sampled times. The estimate is \(\bar{x}=42\) minutes. A new sample would generally give a different estimate; the population parameter and the averaging rule would stay fixed.

Exercise 16.2: Individual times and averages

Suppose delivery times are iid draws from a population with standard deviation \(\sigma=12\) minutes. Compare random samples of 100 and 400 orders.

Compute the standard error of the sample mean for each size. Explain which spread changes and which stays at 12 minutes when the sample size increases.

Solution

The standard errors are \(12/\sqrt{100}=1.2\) minutes and \(12/\sqrt{400}=0.6\) minutes. Quadrupling the sample size halves the spread of sample means across repeated samples. Individual delivery times still have population SD 12 minutes; collecting more orders makes their average more precise without making individual deliveries less variable.

Exercise 16.3: A confidence interval for delivery time

An iid sample of 100 delivery times has sample mean 42 minutes and sample standard deviation 12 minutes. Assume the sampling distribution of the sample mean is approximately normal.

  1. Compute the estimated standard error and an approximate 95% confidence interval for the population mean using the two-SE rule.
  2. A true average delivery time above 45 minutes would trigger a costly process review; is that warranted based on this audit?
  3. Using the sample standard deviation of 12 minutes as a stand-in for the population standard deviation, if the company wanted the margin of error \(2SE(\bar X)\) for the next audit to be 1 minute, how many deliveries should they include? (Hint: set \(2SE(\bar X) = 1\), plug in \(s\) for \(\sigma\), and solve for \(n\).)
Solution
  1. The estimated SE is \(12/\sqrt{100}=1.2\) minutes. The interval is \(42\pm2(1.2)=[39.6,44.4]\) minutes. It gives plausible values for the population mean delivery time under the sampling assumptions.
  2. The interval’s upper endpoint, 44.4 minutes, is below 45 minutes. Under the sampling assumptions, this audit does not support triggering a process review on the grounds that the population mean exceeds 45 minutes.
  3. Using 12 minutes as the planning value for the population standard deviation, we solve \(2(12)/\sqrt n=1\), giving \(\sqrt n=24\) and \(n=576\). The next audit should include at least 576 deliveries to target a margin of error no larger than 1 minute.

16.4 The bootstrap

For the sample mean, \(s/\sqrt n\) estimates the standard error directly. For other statistics, we may not have a convenient formula, and a normal approximation may not describe the sampling distribution well.

The bootstrap estimates sampling variability by repeatedly sampling from the observed data. In the app above we sampled from a known population. Here we use the sample as an approximation to the population distribution, preserving the sample size and computing the statistic again on each resample.

Definition 16.5: The bootstrap

To bootstrap a statistic computed from a sample of size \(n\): draw \(n\) observations from the sample with replacement, compute the statistic on this bootstrap sample, and repeat many times (say \(B = 10{,}000\)).

The distribution of these \(B\) statistics approximates how the statistic varies across samples, and their standard deviation is the bootstrap standard error. This use of the bootstrap relies on an iid sample that represents the population well enough for the statistic we want to estimate.

Why sample n=81 firms?

We want to estimate variability across samples of 81 firms, so each bootstrap resample must also contain 81 firms. Smaller resamples would overstate the variability of the sample mean; larger ones would understate it. The number of resamples, \(B\), controls how precisely the simulation estimates that variability. It does not increase the original sample size.

Why sample with replacement?

Each bootstrap iteration draws 81 firms from a sample of exactly 81 firms. Without replacement there is only one possible draw: every resampled dataset would be the original sample, just shuffled, and the mean wouldn’t vary across bootstrap resamples at all. Sampling with replacement is what lets the resamples differ.

When is the formula useful?

For a sample mean, the formula is quick to compute and shows how precision depends on sample size: quadrupling \(n\) halves the standard error. The bootstrap requires more computation but can estimate standard errors when a formula is inconvenient or unavailable. It can also show skew or other departures from a normal shape. Using the bootstrap to estimate an SE does not, by itself, justify a two-SE confidence interval; that interval still needs an approximately normal sampling distribution centered at the parameter.

Figure 16.9 shows the result of bootstrapping the SAP mean, with a normal curve centered at the observed sample mean and using our estimated SE for comparison.

The bootstrap distribution is centered near the observed sample mean because we resample from the 81 observed firms. The actual sampling distribution is centered at the unknown population mean; the bootstrap approximates its spread using the sample.

Using the bootstrap SE in the two-SE interval gives approximately [7.0%, 18.3%], compared with [6.9%, 18.3%] from the formula. The lower endpoint rounds differently, but the conclusions about the SAP dispute are unchanged.

Technical detail 16.2: Why the two standard errors are so close

Treat the observed sample as fixed. Each bootstrap observation is an independent draw from the distribution that gives each of the \(n\) observed data points probability \(1/n\). That distribution has mean \(\bar x\) and a standard deviation of approximately \(s\) (technically \(\sqrt{\frac{n-1}{n}}s\), about \(0.99s\) for these 81 firms).

Let \(\bar X^*\) be the mean of a bootstrap resample of size \(n\). The CLT applies here too:

\[ \bar X^* \;\approx\; N\!\left(\bar x,\;s^2/n\right). \]

The center is the original sample mean, and the standard deviation is approximately \(s/\sqrt n\). As we increase the number of resamples \(B\), the standard deviation of the simulated means approaches the bootstrap distribution’s standard deviation, approximately \(s/\sqrt n\). Increasing \(B\) makes the simulation more precise; the adequacy of the normal approximation still depends on \(n\) and the shape of the observed-data distribution.

For the sample mean, the bootstrap therefore gives nearly the same two-SE interval as the formula:

\[ \bar x \pm 2\times\text{(bootstrap SE)} \;\approx\; \bar x \pm 2\frac{s}{\sqrt n}. \]

The benefit to using the bootstrap? We could get nearly the same answer as using the CLT without doing any math at all to work out the standard error. For a sample mean the math is not a big impediment, but it can be for other statistics, and sometimes the math requires modeling assumptions we would rather not make.

Exercise 16.4: Check a bootstrap design

An analyst has an iid sample of 100 delivery times and wants a bootstrap standard error for its sample mean. Assume the sample adequately represents the population. The analyst proposes three procedures, each repeated 10,000 times:

  1. Draw 100 observations without replacement and compute their mean.
  2. Draw 400 observations with replacement and compute their mean.
  3. Draw 100 observations with replacement and compute their mean.

Which procedure matches the original sample size and allows the resamples to vary? Explain the problem with each other procedure. In the matching procedure, what quantity should the analyst compute from the 10,000 means to estimate SE?

Solution

Procedure 3 matches the original sample size and uses replacement so that observations can repeat or be omitted. Procedure 1 only shuffles the same 100 observations, so every resample mean is identical. Procedure 2 mimics a sample of 400 rather than 100, understating the variability of the mean for the original sample size. The sample standard deviation of the 10,000 means is the bootstrap SE; it is not the standard deviation of the delivery times within one resample.

16.5 Additional practice

Exercise 16.5: Each company against its own industry

The chapter compared the SAP customers’ mean ROE with the 15.7% average of their industry benchmarks. The sap_roe data also allow a comparison company by company: for each of the 81 firms, subtract its industry’s ROE from its own. Figure 16.10 shows these differences. Their sample mean is -3.06 percentage points and their sample standard deviation is 23.3 points.

  1. State the parameter this analysis estimates, and explain how it differs from the \(\mu\) in Section 16.2.
  2. Compute the estimated standard error and an approximate 95% confidence interval for that parameter.
  3. Is a true average gap of zero, meaning SAP customers match their peers on average, a plausible value? Is Nucleus’s headline gap of about \(-3\) points? SAP’s advertised 32% advantage over peers would put the average gap near \(+5.0\) points; is that plausible?
  4. The standard error here, 2.6 points, is smaller than the standard error of the mean ROE alone, 2.8 points, even though each difference combines two uncertain quantities. Use the variance rule for a difference from Chapter 14 and the sample correlation between a company’s ROE and its industry’s, 0.42, to explain why.
  5. Which comparison better answers “are SAP customers less profitable than their peers”: the chapter’s, which compares the mean ROE with the fixed number 15.7%, or this one? Explain what each treats as fixed and what each treats as random.
Solution
  1. The parameter is the average, over all of SAP’s customers, of each company’s ROE minus its own industry’s ROE. Call it \(\delta\). The chapter’s \(\mu\) is the average ROE of SAP customers on its own; \(\delta\) measures the average gap between each customer and its peers, so it already accounts for the mix of industries the customers come from.
  2. The estimated standard error is \(23.3/\sqrt{81}\approx2.59\) points. The interval is \(-3.1\pm2(2.59) \approx[-8.2,\ 2.1]\) percentage points.
  3. Zero lies inside the interval, so parity with peers is plausible. So is the \(-3\)-point gap Nucleus reported, along with gaps as large as \(-8\) points. A gap of \(+5.0\) points lies above the upper endpoint and is not a plausible value at this confidence level. The data do not support SAP’s claim, and they do not establish Nucleus’s headline either.
  4. A difference has variance \(\operatorname{Var}(X)+\operatorname{Var}(Y)-2\operatorname{Cov}(X,Y)\). Companies in profitable industries tend to have high ROE themselves, and the sample correlation of 0.42 makes the covariance term positive. Subtracting the industry benchmark removes the part of each company’s ROE that is explained by its industry, leaving a difference whose standard deviation, 23.3 points, is below the 25.7 points of ROE alone. The standard error shrinks with it.
  5. The paired comparison is the better answer to the question as posed. The chapter’s version treats 15.7% as a known constant, but it is the average of the 81 sampled companies’ industry benchmarks, and a different sample of 81 customers would have a different industry mix and a different benchmark average. Pairing each company with its own industry makes the benchmark part of the observation, so the sampling variability of the industry mix is included in the standard error rather than ignored.
Exercise 16.6: Reading a confidence interval

A grocery delivery app takes a random sample of 400 orders from last month. The order value is the amount spent on an order. The sample mean order value is $64.20 and the sample standard deviation is $38.00, so the estimated standard error is \(38/\sqrt{400}=1.90\) and the two-SE interval for the mean order value \(\mu\) is \([60.4,\ 68.0]\) dollars.

Decide whether each statement is a correct reading of the interval. Where it is not, say what is wrong and what a correct version would say.

  1. “About 95% of orders have order values between $60.40 and $68.00.”
  2. “There is a 95% probability that the mean order value across all of last month’s orders is between $60.40 and $68.00.”
  3. “If we sampled another 400 orders, there is a 95% chance their sample mean would fall between $60.40 and $68.00.”
  4. “A mean order value of $70 is not a plausible value at this level of confidence.”
  5. “Sampling 1,600 orders instead of 400 would roughly halve the margin of error.”
  6. “The mean order value last month was $64.20.”

Finally, a free-delivery promotion breaks even if the mean order value is at least $62. What does the interval say about whether the promotion breaks even?

Solution
  1. Incorrect. The interval describes plausible values for the mean order value. The sample standard deviation of individual order values is $38, but the interval concerns uncertainty in the mean, not the spread of orders. A correct version: “the mean order value is plausibly anywhere from $60.40 to $68.00.”
  2. This is the common mistake described in Section 16.2.2. The mean is a fixed number, and this particular interval either contains it or does not. The 95% describes the procedure: across repeated random samples of 400 orders, about 95% of intervals built this way contain the true mean.
  3. Incorrect. The interval estimates the population mean; it is not an interval designed to contain the next sample mean with probability 0.95. A new sample mean varies around the unknown population mean, and the probability that it falls inside this particular interval is not given by the confidence level.
  4. Correct. $70 lies above the interval’s upper endpoint, so the observed mean would be unusually far below the true mean if it were $70.
  5. Correct, approximately. The standard error is \(s/\sqrt n\), so quadrupling \(n\) halves it, provided the new sample’s standard deviation is close to $38.
  6. Incorrect as stated. $64.20 is the sample mean, the estimate; the mean of all of last month’s orders is unknown. The interval is the honest report: the best estimate is $64.20, and values within about $3.80 of it cannot be ruled out.

The break-even value of $62 lies inside the interval. The best estimate is above it, but a true mean below $62 is consistent with the data, so the sample cannot rule out a promotion that loses money. A decision needs either a larger sample or a judgment about how costly the downside is.

Exercise 16.7: Sample size, population size, and what the standard error leaves out

A national bank with two million checking customers surveys 800 of them, chosen at random, on a ten-point satisfaction scale. A regional credit union with 20,000 members runs the same survey on 800 randomly chosen members. Both samples have a standard deviation of 2.0 points; the bank’s mean is 7.4 and the credit union’s is 7.9.

  1. Compute the estimated standard error of each sample mean.
  2. A bank executive argues that the credit union’s estimate must be far more precise, because 800 is 4% of its members but only 0.04% of the bank’s. Evaluate the argument.
  3. The bank considers an alternative: email the survey to all two million customers. Suppose 60,000 respond, with a sample standard deviation of 2.0 points. Compute the standard error that formula would give for the mean of those 60,000 responses, and explain what that number does not capture. Which estimate would you trust more, the 800 random customers or the 60,000 volunteers?
  4. Return to SAP’s objection that 81 companies out of 30,000 is too few to say anything. Which fact determines the precision of the Nucleus estimate: the fraction of customers sampled, or the sample size together with the spread of ROE? Then describe a different objection SAP could have raised about the 81 companies, which came from the customer list on SAP’s own website.
Solution
  1. Both standard errors are \(2.0/\sqrt{800}\approx0.071\) points. The formula uses the sample size and the spread of responses, and those are the same in both surveys.
  2. The argument confuses the fraction sampled with the sample size. The sampling model treats the 800 responses as iid draws from the population’s distribution, and the precision of their average depends on how many draws there are and how variable each one is, not on how many customers went unsampled. Sampling without replacement from a smaller population does make the draws slightly dependent in a way that helps the credit union, but at 4% of the population the effect is a reduction of about 2% in the standard error, far from the executive’s “far more precise.”
  3. The formula gives \(2.0/\sqrt{60{,}000}\approx0.008\) points. That number describes sampling variability under the assumption that the 60,000 are a random sample of the two million, and they are not: they are the 3% who chose to answer. If dissatisfied customers respond more often, the mean of the volunteers is biased downward, and no sample size shrinks that bias. The standard error reports only the error that random sampling introduces. The 800 random customers give an estimate whose error the model can describe; the 60,000 volunteers give an estimate whose main error the model ignores.
  4. The precision of the Nucleus estimate depends on \(n=81\) and the spread of ROE, which gave a standard error of about 2.9 points, and not on the fraction 81/30,000. The better objection concerns how the 81 were chosen. Companies listed as customers on SAP’s website are the ones SAP chose to showcase, which need not resemble a random draw from all 30,000. If that list is unrepresentative, the estimate is biased in a direction the standard error cannot reveal, in either direction.
Exercise 16.8: Bootstrapping the headline

Nucleus’s headline, “SAP customers are 20 percent less profitable than their peers,” is a ratio: the sample mean ROE divided by the average industry benchmark, minus one, or \(12.6/15.7-1\approx-19.5\%\). Figure 16.11 shows 10,000 bootstrap values of this percentage difference, each computed from a resample of the 81 companies.

  1. Why does \(s/\sqrt n\) not give the standard error of this statistic?
  2. Describe the bootstrap procedure that produced the figure. Explain why each resample draws whole rows of the data, keeping each company’s ROE together with its industry benchmark, rather than resampling the two columns separately.
  3. The bootstrap standard error is 16.5 percentage points. Form the two-SE interval for the true percentage difference and say which of these are plausible values: parity with peers, Nucleus’s 20% deficit, and SAP’s advertised 32% advantage.
  4. The 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of the bootstrap values are -52.7% and 12.2%. Compare them with the two-SE interval and comment on whether the normal approximation is adequate here.
  5. Compare this conclusion with the one from the paired comparison above, which measured the gap in percentage points rather than as a ratio.
Solution
  1. The statistic is a ratio of two sample means, each of which varies from sample to sample, and the two vary together because a company’s ROE is correlated with its industry’s. The formula \(s/\sqrt n\) describes the sampling variability of a single sample mean and says nothing about how a ratio of two correlated means varies.
  2. Draw 81 companies from the 81 observed ones with replacement, compute the mean ROE and mean benchmark of the resample, form the percentage difference, and repeat 10,000 times. The standard deviation of the 10,000 values is the bootstrap standard error. Resampling rows preserves the pairing of each company with its industry benchmark. The correlation can vary from resample to resample. Resampling the columns separately would break that dependence and describe a different, incorrect sampling process.
  3. The interval is \(-19.5\pm2(16.5) \approx[-52.6\%,\ 13.6\%]\). Parity, at 0%, is inside it, and so is a 20% deficit; the data are also consistent with deficits far larger than Nucleus claimed. A 32% advantage lies well above the upper endpoint and is not plausible.
  4. The percentile interval is close to the two-SE interval, and the bootstrap histogram is roughly bell-shaped with a mild skew, so the normal approximation is adequate for these purposes. The bootstrap has done two jobs here: it supplied a standard error that no simple formula gives, and it showed that the sampling distribution is close enough to normal for the two-SE interval to be reasonable.
  5. Both analyses lead to the same reading of the dispute. Zero gap is plausible, Nucleus’s reported gap is plausible, and SAP’s advertised advantage is not. The substantive conclusion does not depend on which scale we use.
Exercise 16.9: How well do we know the minimum-variance weight?

In Chapter 14, the minimum-variance mix of the DAX and FTSE indices put a weight of 0.173 on the DAX, computed from 1,859 daily returns over 1991 through 1998. That weight is an estimate: a different eight years would have given a different number. Figure 16.12 shows 5,000 bootstrap values of the weight, each computed from a resample of the 1,859 days. For these calculations, use an iid model for the daily pairs of returns; part 2 examines its limitations.

  1. Why is the two-SE interval from Section 16.2.2 not directly available for this statistic? What does the bootstrap supply?
  2. Each bootstrap resample draws days with replacement, keeping each day’s DAX and FTSE returns together. Explain why the pairing must be kept, and what assumption about the days the resampling relies on. Use the daily-returns exercise in Chapter 15 to explain why near-zero correlation between consecutive returns does not establish that assumption.
  3. The bootstrap standard error is 0.043. Form the two-SE interval for the true minimum-variance weight. Is a weight of zero, meaning the FTSE alone, a plausible value? Is an even split?
  4. The 2.5th and 97.5th bootstrap percentiles are 0.088 and 0.258. Compare them with the two-SE interval and with the shape of Figure 16.12.
  5. Write two sentences advising a FTSE investor who asked for the variance-minimizing DAX allocation, conveying both the estimate and its uncertainty.
Solution
  1. The weight is a ratio built from two sample variances and a sample covariance, and there is no simple formula like \(s/\sqrt n\) for its standard error. The bootstrap supplies an estimate of that standard error and a picture of the sampling distribution’s shape, by recomputing the weight on repeated resamples of the observed days.
  2. The weight depends on the covariance between the two indices, which lives in the pairing of each day’s two returns; resampling the columns separately would destroy it. Resampling days assumes the days are independent draws from a common distribution. Near-zero correlation in raw returns does not establish independence: large absolute returns can cluster even when signed returns are uncorrelated. The weight depends on variances and covariance, so that dependence can affect its uncertainty. The interval here is conditional on an iid approximation whose adequacy the earlier exercise does not establish.
  3. The interval is \(0.173\pm2(0.043) \approx[0.09,\ 0.26]\). Under the iid model, zero lies below the interval, so holding the FTSE alone is not a plausible variance-minimizing choice at this confidence level. An even split lies far above it and is not plausible either.
  4. The percentiles nearly coincide with the two-SE endpoints, and the bootstrap histogram is roughly symmetric and bell-shaped. This supports the two-SE approximation within the iid resampling model; it does not check that model’s adequacy for dependent returns.
  5. “Under an iid model for daily returns, the estimated DAX weight is 17%, with a two-SE interval from 9% to 26%. Dependence across days or changes in the return distribution could make that interval unreliable for an allocation decision.”
Exercise 16.10: Mortgage denial rates

The HMDA data in the AER package come from a Federal Reserve Bank of Boston study of 2,380 mortgage applications in the Boston area in 1990. Each application was either denied or approved, and the data record whether the applicant was Black. Table 16.2 gives the counts. Treat the applications as a random sample of mortgage applications in the area that year.

  1. A denial rate is the average of a variable that equals 1 for a denied application and 0 otherwise. Using the variance of a 0/1 variable from the conversion-rate exercise in Chapter 15, write the standard error of a sample proportion \(\hat p\) based on \(n\) applications.
  2. Compute the estimated standard error and a two-SE interval for the denial rate among all applications in the area, and then separately for Black applicants and for other applicants.
  3. The interval for Black applicants is about four times as wide as the interval for other applicants. Give the two reasons.
  4. The difference between the two sample denial rates is 19.1 percentage points. Treating the two groups as independent samples, the standard error of the difference is \(\sqrt{SE_1^2+SE_2^2}\). Explain where that formula comes from, compute it, and form a two-SE interval for the difference in denial rates.
  5. State carefully what the interval in part 4 does and does not establish. What questions would the Boston Fed’s researchers have needed to answer before interpreting the gap?
Solution
  1. A 0/1 variable with mean \(p\) has variance \(p(1-p)\), so the average of \(n\) of them has standard deviation \(\sqrt{p(1-p)/n}\). Plugging in the sample proportion gives the estimated standard error \(\sqrt{\hat p(1-\hat p)/n}\).
  2. Overall, \(\hat p=0.120\) with standard error \(\sqrt{0.120(0.880)/2,380}\approx0.0067\), for an interval of about \([10.6\%,\ 13.3\%]\). For Black applicants, \(\hat p=0.283\) with standard error 0.024 and interval \([23.4\%,\ 33.2\%]\). For other applicants, \(\hat p=0.093\) with standard error 0.006 and interval \([8.0\%,\ 10.5\%]\).
  3. The sample of Black applicants is about one sixth the size, which by itself multiplies the standard error by about \(\sqrt6\approx2.4\). Their denial rate is also closer to one half, where \(p(1-p)\) is largest, which adds the rest of the difference.
  4. The difference of two independent estimators has variance equal to the sum of their variances, by the variance rule for a difference with zero covariance; taking the square root gives the formula. Here the standard error is \(\sqrt{0.024^2+0.006^2}\approx0.025\), and the interval is about \([14.0,\ 24.1]\) percentage points.
  5. Under the sampling model, the interval excludes a zero gap by a wide margin, so random sampling variation alone is an implausible explanation for the observed difference. It does not say why the gap exists. Applicants differ in income, debt ratios, credit history, and the properties they sought, and the raw rates make no adjustment for any of that. The Boston Fed study’s central question was whether the gap survives after accounting for those characteristics, which requires the tools of the regression chapters. The interval here answers only the question of whether the raw gap could be sampling noise.
Exercise 16.11: Two stores, two intervals

A retailer audits average transaction size at two stores. At Store A, a random sample of 64 transactions has mean $52 and standard deviation $16. At Store B, a random sample of 100 transactions has mean $58 and standard deviation $20.

  1. Compute the two-SE interval for each store’s mean transaction size.
  2. The two intervals overlap. The regional manager concludes that “the stores could easily have the same average, so there is no evidence of a difference.” Before computing anything more, say whether overlapping intervals justify that conclusion.
  3. The estimated difference in means is $6. Treating the samples as independent, compute the standard error of the difference and a two-SE interval for the true difference in mean transaction size.
  4. Reconcile parts 1 and 3. Why can two intervals overlap while the interval for their difference excludes zero?
  5. What does the interval in part 3 say about the size of the difference, as opposed to its existence? How would you report the result?
Solution
  1. Store A’s standard error is \(16/\sqrt{64}=2\), giving \([48,\ 56]\). Store B’s is \(20/\sqrt{100}=2\), giving \([54,\ 62]\).
  2. No. Each interval describes plausible values for one mean on its own. Whether the two means could be equal is a question about their difference, which has its own sampling distribution and its own interval. Overlap is a rough visual check that is too conservative: it can fail to flag differences the data actually establish.
  3. The difference of two independent estimators has variance equal to the sum of their variances, so the standard error is \(\sqrt{2^2+2^2}\approx2.83\). The interval is \(6\pm2(2.83)\), about \([0.3,\ 11.7]\) dollars.
  4. Two intervals fail to overlap only when the gap between the means exceeds the sum of the two margins, \(2(2)+2(2)=8\) here. The interval for the difference excludes zero when the gap exceeds two standard errors of the difference, \(2\sqrt{2^2+2^2}\approx5.7\). The second threshold is smaller because standard errors add in quadrature, not directly. A gap of $6 clears the correct threshold and not the overlap test.
  5. The interval says the true difference is plausibly anywhere from about 30 cents to nearly $12. The direction is reasonably well established, but the size is not: the data are consistent with a difference too small to act on and with one large enough to matter. A fair report gives the estimate of $6 and the interval together, rather than either “the stores differ” or “no evidence of a difference.”
Exercise 16.12: Estimating a total from a sample of invoices

An auditor reviews a company’s 8,000 supplier invoices for the year. Checking every one is too costly, so the auditor draws 200 at random without replacement and records the overbilling on each, in dollars, with zero for a correct invoice. The sampled overbilling has mean $12.40 and standard deviation $60. Only 60 of the 200 invoices contained any error. For parts 1–3, use the iid approximation: 200 invoices are only 2.5% of the population.

  1. Compute the estimated standard error and a two-SE interval for the mean overbilling per invoice across all 8,000.
  2. The quantity that matters is the total overbilling, which is 8,000 times the mean. Give an estimate of the total and a two-SE interval for it. Which rule from the probability chapters lets you scale the interval this way?
  3. The company treats total overbilling above $50,000 as material. What does the interval say about whether the total is material?
  4. The auditor wants the margin of error for the total to be $20,000. First use $60 as the planning standard deviation and \(s/\sqrt n\) to find a sample size. This would sample a substantial fraction of the invoices. For sampling without replacement from \(N=8{,}000\), the estimated SE of the mean is \(\dfrac{s}{\sqrt n}\sqrt{1-\dfrac nN}\). Use this correction to find the smallest whole-number sample size with a two-SE margin of at most $20,000 for the total.
  5. Seventy percent of the sampled invoices had zero error, so the overbilling distribution is a spike at zero with a right tail. Does that shape threaten the interval in part 1? Say what would make you more or less comfortable with it.
Solution
  1. The standard error is \(60/\sqrt{200}\approx4.24\) dollars, and the interval is \(12.40\pm2(4.24)\), about \([3.9,\ 20.9]\) dollars per invoice.
  2. The estimated total is \(8{,}000(12.40)=99{,}200\) dollars. Multiplying an estimator by a constant multiplies its standard error by the same constant, so the total’s standard error is \(8{,}000(4.24)\approx33{,}900\) and the interval is about \([31{,}000,\ 167{,}000]\) dollars. This is the scaling rule for the standard deviation of \(aX\).
  3. The interval spans the threshold. A total below $50,000 is plausible, and so is one three times the threshold. The sample does not settle materiality; the best estimate is well above the line, but the uncertainty is too large to conclude either way.
  4. A margin of $20,000 on the total is $2.50 on the mean. The iid calculation \(2(60)/\sqrt n=2.5\) gives \(n=2{,}304\), more than a quarter of the population. With the correction, squaring \(2(60)\sqrt{1/n-1/8{,}000}\le2.5\) gives \(n\ge 2{,}304/(1+2{,}304/8{,}000)\approx1{,}788.82\). Round up to 1,789 invoices. Sampling without replacement reduces uncertainty because each additional invoice leaves fewer unchecked.
  5. If a few invoices have very large errors, the sample mean and standard deviation both depend heavily on whether those invoices were drawn. A sample that misses them can underestimate both the mean and its uncertainty. Inspecting the largest sampled errors or bootstrapping the mean can reveal sensitivity to the observed tail, but neither can reveal extreme errors absent from the sample. Information about such errors in the full invoice population would help assess the interval.
Exercise 16.13: CEO pay and the uncertainty in a standard deviation

The ceosal1 data in the wooldridge package are a random sample of 209 chief executives’ 1990 salaries, in thousands of dollars, drawn from a Businessweek survey. The sample mean is $1,281 thousand, the median is $1,039 thousand, the standard deviation is $1,372 thousand, and the largest salary is $14,822 thousand. We bootstrapped the mean, the median, and the standard deviation, 10,000 resamples each.

  1. Compute the formula standard error \(s/\sqrt n\) for the mean salary and the two-SE interval. Compare the standard error with the bootstrap standard error in Table 16.3.
  2. The median’s bootstrap standard error is about half the mean’s. Explain why the median is estimated more precisely than the mean in this sample.
  3. The bootstrap interval for the standard deviation runs from about $608 thousand to $2,020 thousand. Explain why the standard deviation is so uncertain, and what that means for any calculation in the earlier chapters that treated a sample standard deviation as if it were the population value.
  4. A compensation committee asks what a typical CEO in this population earned. Which summary would you give them, with what interval, and what would you say about the population the sample represents?
Solution
  1. The formula gives \(1,372/\sqrt{209}\approx95\) thousand dollars, and the interval \([1,091,\ 1,471]\). The bootstrap standard error of 94 is nearly identical, as the technical note on the bootstrap for the mean in Section 16.4 predicts.
  2. The mean moves whenever the largest salaries are included or excluded from a resample; the median depends only on the middle of the ordered sample and barely notices the tail. In a heavily skewed sample the median is both a more stable and a more representative summary of a typical value.
  3. The standard deviation is dominated by the squared distance of the largest salary from the mean. Whether that one CEO appears zero, one, or several times in a resample changes \(s\) by hundreds of thousands of dollars, so \(s\) is estimated with a relative uncertainty of roughly 30%. Every calculation that plugged \(s\) in for \(\sigma\), including the standard errors in this chapter, inherits that uncertainty; the effect is small when the data are well behaved and large when a few extreme values drive \(s\).
  4. The median, about $1,039 thousand with a bootstrap interval of roughly $937 to $1,115 thousand, describes a typical CEO better than the mean, which a handful of very large salaries pull upward. The interval concerns the population represented by the magazine’s survey of 1990 salaries. Generalizing to all CEOs, or to a different year, would require evidence that the survey represents that population.
