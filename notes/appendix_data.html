Data Documentation

This appendix documents every dataset used in these notes: what it is, where it came from, and the variables that appear in the examples. Each chapter ends with a “Data used in this section” list linking back to the relevant entries here. The raw files live in the book’s data/ directory.

A.1 Austin House Listings

This dataset contains listing-level information on residential properties in Austin, Texas scraped from Zillow and assembled by Eric Pierce. Variables include sale price, structural characteristics, lot and living area, school proximity metrics, and free-text listing descriptions. The data are used here to predict home sale prices from a property’s attributes — both structured variables such as square footage, bedroom count, and neighborhood school ratings, and unstructured listing descriptions encoded as dense text embeddings.

Resources:

  • Kaggle dataset

Provenance. The data were scraped from Zillow by Eric Pierce and published on Kaggle in 2021. Coverage is limited to Austin, Texas residential listings with recorded sale prices. The dataset is distributed via Kaggle without a formal license; access requires a Kaggle account. See Pierce (2021) for the primary citation.

Variables

Text embeddings

The *_embeddings.csv files append 1,536 numeric columns (dim_1 through dim_1536) representing dense vector embeddings of each listing’s description field. Embeddings were generated using OpenAI’s text-embedding-3-small model and retrieved on 2026-06-23. Listings with missing or empty descriptions are excluded from the embeddings file and appear with NA in the embedding columns after the left join on zpid. The embeddings are used as predictors in lasso (lasso.R) and random forest (rf.R) models to assess the marginal predictive value of listing text beyond structural variables.

A.2 CPS ASEC Earnings

The Annual Social and Economic Supplement (ASEC) to the Current Population Survey (CPS) is a nationally representative household survey conducted each March by the U.S. Census Bureau and Bureau of Labor Statistics. It is one of the principal federal sources for data on annual earnings, employment, and hours worked in the United States. The survey covers civilian non-institutional residents and collects detailed information on labor force participation, earnings, education, and demographics.

This dataset contains information on wages, age, education, and gender for a subsample of prime-age, full-time, full-year wage earners extracted from the Current Population Survey (CPS). The CPS is the primary source for labor market data in the U.S.; its best-known use is in estimating the unemployment rate and related statistics. It is widely used in economics research, including studies of wage determination and human capital.

Each March the CPS includes the Annual Social and Economic Supplement (ASEC), which collects detailed information on income, earnings, and work experience for the prior calendar year. The ASEC is the most comprehensive source of data on annual earnings in the U.S. This dataset was extracted from the 2023 CPS ASEC.

Resources:

  • CPS ASEC documentation (Census Bureau) – details about the survey design, target population, data collection, and variables available in the ASEC.
  • Mincer earnings function (Wikipedia) – this dataset is suitable for estimating the Mincer earnings function, a widely used model in labor economics that relates wages to schooling and experience.

Data were obtained via the cpsR R package, which queries the Census Bureau’s API and returns a tidy extract from the 2023 ASEC public-use microdata file. The underlying microdata represent civilian non-institutional respondents nationwide. No license restrictions apply to CPS public-use files; the data are freely available from the U.S. Census Bureau.

A.2.2 Sample filters

The following filters were applied sequentially in fetch_2023.R:

  1. Full-year workers: weeks_worked > 45 — retains respondents who worked more than 45 weeks in the prior year.
  2. Full-time workers: hrs_per_week > 35 — retains respondents who usually worked more than 35 hours per week.
  3. Positive wage earners: wage_income > 0 — drops respondents with zero or missing wage and salary income.
  4. Valid age: age < 100 — excludes top-coded or implausible age values.
  5. Non-children: educ_code != 0 — explicitly excludes child records for which educational attainment is not reported.
  6. Valid education recode: !is.na(educ_years) — drops any remaining records where the recode produced a missing value.
  7. Internally consistent age–education: age >= educ_years + 6 — requires that age is at least as large as the minimum age at which a person could have completed the stated level of schooling (school entry at age 6).
  8. Minimum working age: age >= 21 — restricts the sample to respondents aged 21 and older, ensuring that the derived experience variable is non-negative for all education levels.

This dataset is cited as U.S. Census Bureau and Bureau of Labor Statistics (2023).

A.3 Weekly Asset Returns (2023–2025)

These data document weekly simple returns for 25 exchange-traded assets—individual equities, a sector ETF, bond ETFs, and a commodity ETF—alongside a contemporaneous weekly risk-free rate, covering the period September 30, 2023 to September 29, 2025. Prices are sourced from Yahoo Finance and converted to weekly simple returns using split- and dividend-adjusted closing prices. The dataset supports analysis of asset pricing and portfolio diversification: the companion script estimates the Capital Asset Pricing Model for each security and examines mean-variance tradeoffs across asset classes and holding-period horizons.

The data ship as two files. returns.csv contains the weekly returns and the risk-free rate only. returns_prices.csv contains the identical rows plus the weekly adjusted closing price for every asset, in columns named <ticker>_price (with SPY’s price as market_price); it supports exercises that contrast price levels with returns, such as the Ford-versus-Tesla comparison in the transformations chapter of the course notes.

Provenance. Adjusted daily closing prices were retrieved from Yahoo Finance via the tidyquant R package. The data request covers September 30, 2023 to September 29, 2025 (start_date and end_date as set in data_extract.R). The risk-free rate is proxied by the 13-week U.S. Treasury bill secondary-market yield (^IRX), converted from an annualized percentage to a weekly decimal rate by dividing by 100 and then by 52; forward-filling patches any holiday gaps in that series. Price columns in returns_prices.csv record the last available split- and dividend-adjusted daily close on or before each weekly return date, so prices and returns are aligned on the same Friday grid. Yahoo Finance terms of use restrict redistribution of raw price data; this assembled dataset is intended for educational use. See Weekly Asset Return Panel, October 2023–September 2025 (2025) for retrieval details.

A.4 Three-Asset Portfolio Series (2023–2025)

These data document weekly simple returns and adjusted prices for three exchange-traded funds—a U.S. equity market fund (SPY), an international developed-markets equity fund (EFA), and a long-term U.S. Treasury bond fund (TLT)—alongside a contemporaneous weekly risk-free rate, covering the period September 30, 2023 to September 29, 2025. The three funds were chosen for the portfolio construction material in the course notes: over this window they had nearly identical volatility but quite different mean returns, and the two equity funds are strongly correlated with each other while the bond fund is nearly uncorrelated with both. Prices are sourced from Yahoo Finance and converted to weekly simple returns using split- and dividend-adjusted closing prices.

The dataset ships as a single file, portfolios.csv, containing the weekly returns, the risk-free rate, and the aligned weekly adjusted closing prices. The rows sit on the same Friday grid as the broader weekly asset returns panel (Section A.3), so the two datasets can be joined on date; the SPY column here is the same series as that dataset’s market column.

Provenance. Adjusted daily closing prices were retrieved from Yahoo Finance via the tidyquant R package. The data request covers September 30, 2023 to September 29, 2025 (start_date and end_date as set in data_extract.R). The risk-free rate is proxied by the 13-week U.S. Treasury bill secondary-market yield (^IRX), converted from an annualized percentage to a weekly decimal rate by dividing by 100 and then by 52; forward-filling patches any holiday gaps in that series. Price columns record the last available split- and dividend-adjusted daily close on or before each weekly return date, so prices and returns are aligned on the same Friday grid. Yahoo Finance terms of use restrict redistribution of raw price data; this assembled dataset is intended for educational use. See Three-Asset Weekly Return and Price Series, October 2023–September 2025 (2025) for retrieval details.

A.5 ERCOT Hourly Load and Temperature Panel

These data were assembled to characterize the relationship between temperature and electricity demand in Texas. The panel links hourly zone-level load from ERCOT with hourly surface temperature observations from nearby weather stations, enabling temperature response functions to be estimated separately for each of ERCOT’s eight weather zones. Shaffer (2022) uses these data to examine how the load–temperature relationship changed over time, estimating the functions across rolling multi-year windows from 2002 through 2020.

Resources:

  • Article
  • Replication archive

Provenance. Load data originate from the Electric Reliability Council of Texas (ERCOT) and reflect hourly settlement-point electricity demand, in megawatts, for each of ERCOT’s eight weather zones from January 2002 through March 2021. Temperature data originate from NOAA’s Local Climatological Data (LCD) product, which reports hourly observations from Automated Surface Observing System (ASOS) and Automated Weather Observing System (AWOS) stations. NOAA LCD reports dry-bulb temperature in degrees Fahrenheit for U.S. stations; the field name HourlyDryBulbTemperature is the exact column name used in LCD exports. One representative station was selected per ERCOT weather zone. The two sources are matched on datetime and zone. Large-scale load-shed events (outages) are flagged from ERCOT operating records to allow their exclusion from estimation. All data were curated by Blake Shaffer and are archived at the replication repository above. See Shaffer (2022) for the primary citation.

A.6 LendingClub Accepted Loans

LendingClub operated an online marketplace that matched individual borrowers with investors who funded loans in small fractional shares (“notes”). To let investors screen and price loans, the company published detailed loan-level records for every funded loan — borrower credit attributes recorded at origination together with the loan’s subsequent repayment history. These data are widely used to study consumer-credit default and loan pricing. This card documents a cleaned extract used to model loan charge-off and investor payoff.

Resources:

  • Kaggle dataset

Provenance. The underlying data were released by LendingClub Corporation (San Francisco, CA) as public loan-performance files covering U.S. consumer loans issued 2007–2018 Q4, and were compiled into a single Kaggle dataset by Nathan George (user wordsforthewise). This extract uses only the accepted/funded loans and restricts to loans issued 2008–2012 that had reached a terminal status — Fully Paid or Charged Off (92,902 loans; 15.4% charged off). It retains 45 analysis-relevant columns and adds a small number of derived fields (below). Two files share this schema: df_all.csv (all purposes) and df_subs.csv, which further restricts to three purposes (debt_consolidation, home_improvement, small_business). Data are available at the Kaggle dataset page linked above; consult that page for current license terms. See George (2019) for the primary citation.

Variables

Columns are grouped by role. Targets are modeled outcomes; payment record fields are realized after origination and are used only to construct the payoff targets (never as predictors of default). Derived fields (created in the extract) are labeled and defined in terms of their source columns.

A.7 SAP Customer ROE

In 2005 SAP began running advertisements claiming that companies running SAP software are 32% more profitable than companies that don’t, citing a study it had commissioned from Stratascope Inc. that was never released for public scrutiny (Carr 2005). In March 2006 the technology analyst firm Nucleus Research pushed back with a research note titled “SAP customers are 20 percent less profitable than their peers.” Nucleus compiled the publicly traded companies appearing on SAP’s own web site — 81 firms — looked up each one’s return on equity (ROE) from published financial data, and compared it with the average ROE of the firm’s industry. The average across the 81 firms was 12.6%, against an industry-average benchmark of 15.7%. These data are used in the notes to estimate the mean ROE among SAP customers, to test hypotheses about both companies’ claims, and to examine what a non-random sample can and cannot support.

Resources:

  • Nucleus Research note G12 (PDF)
  • Rough Type on the ad campaign
  • Rough Type on the Stratascope study

Provenance. The figures are transcribed from Table 1 of Nucleus Research (2006) (Nucleus Research note G12, March 2006), which lists each company with its SAP solution(s), industry, ROE, and industry-average ROE. Nucleus reports gathering the ROE figures from Bloomberg and the industry averages from Hoovers via Yahoo! Finance. The note itself circulates today courtesy of Oracle — an SAP competitor with an obvious interest in its conclusions — though Nucleus states the analysis was conducted independently and not commissioned by any company. The one derived column, roe_diff, was added for this book.

A caution on sampling. These 81 firms are not a random sample of SAP customers: they are the publicly traded companies SAP itself chose to feature on its web site. The inference chapter leans on this fact — analyses of these data describe SAP’s showcased customers, not SAP customers in general.

A.8 Kroger Packaged Cheese Sales

These data record weekly sales of packaged, sliced American cheese at Kroger grocery stores across 11 U.S. cities. They were assembled by James Scott for use as a teaching dataset in Data Science in R: A Gentle Introduction, where they first appear in the Plots chapter to illustrate exploratory data visualization before being revisited in later chapters on regression with dummy variables. The data are used throughout to examine how price and in-store promotional displays relate to cheese sales volume.

Resources:

  • Data Science in R: A Gentle Introduction

Provenance. The data were compiled by James Scott (University of Texas at Austin) for instructional use and distributed with Data Science in R: A Gentle Introduction (bookdown, last updated August 2021). The dataset covers 11 U.S. cities — Atlanta, Birmingham, Cincinnati, Columbus, Dallas, Detroit, Houston, Indianapolis, Louisville, Nashville, and Roanoke — with 706 observations. No formal license is stated; the data are made available for educational use through the book’s online companion. See Scott (2021) for the primary citation.

A.9 Bloom, Han & Liang (2024) Hybrid WFH Experiment

Bloom, Han & Liang (2024) ran a six-month randomized controlled trial at a Chinese technology company in 2021–2022, randomly assigning 1,612 graduate-educated employees to a hybrid work-from-home schedule (two days per week at home) or a full-time in-person control condition. The study examines whether hybrid WFH affects employee retention, job performance, promotion rates, and subjective productivity beliefs. The three datasets documented here cover the paper’s core outcomes: employee attrition, daily lines of code written by software engineers, and manager perceptions of WFH’s effect on productivity.

Resources:

  • Article
  • Replication archive

Provenance. Data were collected by the research team of Nicholas Bloom, Ruobing Han, and James Liang as part of a pre-registered field experiment conducted at a large Chinese technology firm between July 2021 and June 2022. The replication archive, including the datasets documented here, is deposited at the Harvard Dataverse (doi:10.7910/DVN/6X4ZZL) and is freely available under a Creative Commons CC BY 4.0 licence. The original article was published open-access in Nature in June 2024. See Bloom et al. (2024) for the primary citation.

Attrition

The attrition dataset (attrition.csv) contains one row per employee (n = 1,612) and records whether each individual left the firm during the study period, together with the key subgroup characteristics used to stratify comparisons in the paper.

Productivity

The productivity dataset (codelines.csv) contains one row per engineer-day observation (n ≈ 95,500) and records the daily lines of code written by software engineer employees, together with several log-transformed versions used in the analysis. Zero values are common, reflecting non-coding days.

Manager Perceptions

The perceptions dataset (perceptions.csv) contains one row per survey respondent-wave observation (n ≈ 2,660) and records employees’ self-reported beliefs about how working from home affects their productivity, collected at baseline (before the policy) and endline (after six months). The paper focuses on whether managers updated their perceptions over the course of the experiment.

We use the Bloom, Han & Liang (2024) hybrid WFH experimental data (Bloom et al. 2024); variable definitions and provenance are in Section A.9.

A.10 Household economics (SHED)

The Survey of Household Economics and Decisionmaking (SHED) is an annual survey of U.S. adults conducted since 2013 by the Board of Governors of the Federal Reserve System. It measures how households assess their own finances and the broader economy, alongside employment, income, housing, and demographic characteristics. The course extract shed_2025.csv covers the 2025 wave: 12,934 respondents, drawn from a probability-based online panel designed to be representative of the U.S. adult population.

Resources:

  • SHED — Board of Governors of the Federal Reserve System
  • Public-use data and codebooks

Provenance. The public-use 2025 microdata file was downloaded from the Board’s SHED data page. As a work of the federal government, the file is in the public domain and may be copied and distributed without permission; the Board requests citation. The course extract keeps nine columns from the original 380, renames them to snake_case, and changes no values. Survey weights are not carried in the extract; course examples report unweighted proportions, which on the extract’s variables differ from weighted ones by less than a percentage point. Respondents rate every retained item on a forced-choice basis, so the extract has no missing values and no “don’t know” category.

A.11 Course Evaluations and Instructor Appearance

Do better-looking instructors get better teaching evaluations? Hamermesh and Parker (2005) collected student course evaluations for 463 course sections taught by 94 instructors at the University of Texas at Austin between 2000 and 2002, along with a measure of each instructor’s physical attractiveness: a panel of six students rated a photograph of each instructor, and the ratings were averaged and normalized. Hamermesh and Parker found that instructors rated as better looking receive systematically higher evaluations. In the regression chapters these data serve as a running multiple-regression example — in particular, whether the association between beauty ratings and evaluation scores survives adjusting for instructor age and other course and instructor characteristics.

Resources:

  • Article
  • AER package on CRAN (distributes this extract as TeachingRatings)

Provenance. The data were collected by Daniel Hamermesh and Amy Parker for Hamermesh and Parker (2005). Each row is one course section; instructor characteristics (age, gender, beauty rating, and so on) repeat across an instructor’s sections, indexed by prof. The beauty score is the average of six student panelists’ ratings of the instructor’s photograph, normalized to have mean zero across instructors (standard deviation about 0.79 in this file). This extract is the widely used teaching version of the data that circulates with Stock and Watson’s econometrics text and is distributed in the R package AER as TeachingRatings; the one column not in that distribution, generation, is a recode of age added for teaching purposes. See Hamermesh and Parker (2005) for the primary citation.
