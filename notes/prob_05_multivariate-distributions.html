10  Joint, Marginal, and Conditional Distributions

We want to understand the relationship between default risk and loan purpose. That relationship is an important input into the business decision of whether to write a loan and how to set its terms. Which loan purposes have the greatest default risk?

10.1 Cross-tabulations and contingency tables

We’ll start with counts, because everything else in this chapter is a count divided by some total.

Definition 10.1: Cross-tabulation

A cross-tabulation or “crosstab” — also called a contingency table — counts how many observations fall into each combination of two variables’ categories. It has one row per category of the first variable, one column per category of the second, and a count in every cell. Its margins, the row and column of totals, are the frequency tables for the individual variables.

Fourteen purposes make for a bulky table. We want every calculation in this section small enough to check by hand, so we’ll narrow to the 58,456 loans with three of the more interesting purposes: debt consolidation, home improvement, and small business. Table 10.1 shows their crosstab with loan status.

Look at the margins first, the row and column labeled Total. The Total column on the right is a frequency table of purpose — the same one-variable summary we built earlier, computed on this subset — and the bottom row is the frequency table of loan status: 48,786 fully paid, 9,670 charged off. The interior cells record how the two variables tend to move together.

With two variables there are three different totals we could divide each count by, and each choice answers a different question. Take the cell for small-business loans that charged off. Dividing it by the grand total gives the share of all loans that are small business and charged off:

\[ \frac{822}{58,456} = 0.014. \]

Definition 10.2: Joint proportion

A joint proportion divides an interior cell of a crosstab by the grand total. It is the share of all observations with that combination of categories. The collection of all the joint proportions is the joint distribution of the two variables.

Dividing the small-business row total — the two cells in that row added together — by the grand total gives the share of all loans that are small business:

\[ \frac{2,369 + 822}{58,456} = \frac{3,191}{58,456} = 0.055. \]

Definition 10.3: Marginal proportion

A marginal proportion divides a row or column total by the grand total. It gives one category’s share without separating the observations by the other variable. Equivalently, we can add the joint proportions across the corresponding row or column.

For small-business loans, the marginal proportion is the sum of the two joint proportions in that row:

\[ 0.041 + 0.014 = 0.055. \]

The crosstab has everything we need to answer the opening question: which loan purposes have the greatest default risk? To compute the small-business default rate, the only relevant information is about small-business loans, so we ignore the other rows and divide by the small-business loan total rather than the grand total. From the counts in Table 10.1, 822 of the 3,191 small-business loans charged off:

\[ \frac{822}{3,191} = 0.258. \]

Definition 10.4: Conditional proportion

A conditional proportion divides a cell by its row or column total rather than the grand total. It is the share in one category of one variable among observations in one category of the other. The conditional proportions across all categories of the variable being summarized form its conditional distribution.

The direction of the conditioning matters. Among small-business loans, the share that charged off is 0.258. Among charged-off loans, the share issued to small businesses is 0.085. Both calculations begin with the same cell but divide by different totals.

Table 10.2 collects the default rates for all three purposes.

Exercise 10.1: Hybrid work and attrition

Bloom, Han, and Liang randomly assigned 1,612 employees to a control schedule or a hybrid schedule with two work-from-home days per week (Bloom et al. 2024). During the study, the following employees stayed at or left the firm:

Assigned schedule Stayed Left Total
Control 761 59 820
Hybrid WFH 754 38 792
Total 1,515 97 1,612
  1. Choose one employee at random from the study. Find the joint probability that the employee was assigned to Hybrid WFH and left the firm.
  2. Find the marginal distribution of assigned schedule.
  3. Find the marginal distribution of attrition status.
  4. Find the conditional distribution of attrition status within each assigned schedule. Check that each conditional distribution sums to one.
  5. Find \(\Pr(\text{Hybrid WFH}\mid\text{Left})\).
  6. Explain why the answer to part 5 differs from \(\Pr(\text{Left}\mid\text{Hybrid WFH})\). Name the denominator in each calculation.
Solution

The Hybrid WFH/Left cell contains 38 of the 1,612 employees, so its joint probability is

\[ \frac{38}{1612}=0.0236. \]

The row totals give the marginal distribution of assigned schedule:

\[ \Pr(\text{Control})=\frac{820}{1612}=0.5087, \qquad \Pr(\text{Hybrid WFH})=\frac{792}{1612}=0.4913. \]

The column totals give the marginal distribution of attrition status:

\[ \Pr(\text{Stayed})=\frac{1515}{1612}=0.9398, \qquad \Pr(\text{Left})=\frac{97}{1612}=0.0602. \]

Within the control group, the conditional distribution of attrition status is

\[ \Pr(\text{Stayed}\mid\text{Control})=\frac{761}{820}=0.9280, \qquad \Pr(\text{Left}\mid\text{Control})=\frac{59}{820}=0.0720. \]

Within the Hybrid WFH group, the corresponding distribution is

\[ \Pr(\text{Stayed}\mid\text{Hybrid WFH})=\frac{754}{792}=0.9520, \qquad \Pr(\text{Left}\mid\text{Hybrid WFH})=\frac{38}{792}=0.0480. \]

Each pair sums to one because every employee in that schedule either stayed or left. Reversing the condition gives

\[ \Pr(\text{Hybrid WFH}\mid\text{Left})=\frac{38}{97}=0.3918. \]

The two conditional probabilities share the same numerator, 38 employees, but use different denominators. The first conditions on the 97 employees who left; the second conditions on the 792 employees assigned to Hybrid WFH.

10.2 From crosstabs to joint distributions

Counts depend on the overall sample size. Dividing every count by the grand total normalizes the table: the interior cells now sum to one. A proportion and its percentage are the same number — 0.014 is 1.4% — and from here on we’ll write everything as proportions.

Table 10.3 shows the result of dividing every count in Table 10.1 by the grand total, 58,456.

The interior cells are the joint proportions we computed from the count table. The margins are the corresponding one-variable distributions. As we saw above, the marginal proportion of small-business loans is the sum of the two joint proportions in that row:

\[ 0.041 + 0.014 = 0.055. \]

The fully-paid and charged-off small-business loans together account for 0.055 of all the loans in the table.

The normalized table also gives the conditional proportion. Dividing the joint proportion for “small business and charged off” by the marginal proportion of small-business loans gives the same default rate we computed from counts. Because the table is rounded to three decimals, we carry one extra digit in the calculation:

\[ \frac{0.0141}{0.0546} = 0.258. \]

A conditional proportion is therefore the joint proportion divided by the marginal proportion of the conditioning variable. Dividing count by count or proportion by proportion gives the same answer because the grand total cancels.

This normalized crosstab is the observed-data version of the joint probability tables in the next section. The same correspondence held for one-variable distributions in Section 7.4: probabilities for future outcomes follow the same structure as proportions in observed data. Here it extends to two variables.

10.3 A probability model for future loans

So far we have read Table 10.3 as a record of what happened to 58,456 historical loans with these three purposes. If those loans are representative of future loans with the same purposes, the table gives estimated probabilities for a future loan drawn from this restricted population.

Definition 10.5: Joint probability distribution

For two discrete random variables, a joint probability distribution assigns a probability to every pair of outcomes. Laid out as a table — one variable in the rows, the other in the columns — it follows the same rules as any probability distribution: every cell is non-negative, and all the cells together sum to one.

Let \(P\) code the purpose of a future loan from this restricted population: \(P=1\) for debt consolidation, \(P=2\) for home improvement, and \(P=3\) for small business. The numbers are labels, not quantities; they let us represent a categorical outcome with a numerical random variable. Let \(D\) be the loan’s eventual default indicator. We will use the historical relative frequencies as a probability model for that loan: the probability assigned to each purpose–outcome pair is its count divided by 58,456. For example,

\[ \Pr(\text{small business and default}) = \Pr(P = 3 \text{ and } D = 1) = \frac{822}{58,456} = 0.014. \]

Dividing each of the six interior counts by 58,456 produces the complete joint distribution in Table 10.4.

The six cells cover every possible purpose–outcome pair in this model, and their probabilities add to 1. This is Rule 2 from Section 7.2: one of these pairs must occur.

Rule 3 lets us add probabilities for mutually exclusive pairs. In this model, a loan can default as a debt-consolidation, home-improvement, or small-business loan, so

\[ \begin{aligned} \Pr(D = 1) &= \Pr(P = 1 \text{ and } D = 1) + \Pr(P = 2 \text{ and } D = 1) + \Pr(P = 3 \text{ and } D = 1) \\ &= 0.139 + 0.012 + 0.014 \\ &= 0.165. \end{aligned} \]

The probability of default is therefore the sum of the three joint probabilities in the default column.

Definition 10.6: Marginal probability distribution

A marginal probability distribution gives the probability distribution of one variable on its own. We obtain it from a joint distribution by summing over all possible outcomes of the other variable.

Adding the row and column sums to Table 10.4 gives both marginal distributions.

These one-variable distributions appear in the margins of the joint table, which is where the name marginal comes from. For example, summing the two small-business joint probabilities gives its marginal probability:

\[ \Pr(P = 3) = 0.041 + 0.014 = 0.055. \]

The marginal distribution of \(D\) answers “what is the default probability for a future loan with one of these three purposes before we know which one?” But it deliberately averages over purposes, and we know from Table 10.2 that purpose matters. The sharper question is conditional: given that the loan is for a small business, what is the chance it defaults?

10.3.1 Conditional probability

Definition 10.7: Conditional probability

For events \(A\) and \(B\) with \(\Pr(B) > 0\), the conditional probability of \(A\) given \(B\) is

\[ \Pr(A \mid B) = \frac{\Pr(A \text{ and } B)}{\Pr(B)}. \]

The bar “\(\mid\)” reads “given that” or “conditional on.” To condition on \(B\), keep only the probability that is consistent with \(B\) and rescale it so it sums to one again. That is what dividing the joint probability by the marginal probability of \(B\) does.

The formula is exactly the joint-over-marginal arithmetic we did with proportions in Section 10.2, now stated as a definition.

On the table, conditioning is a two-step procedure: keep the row or column consistent with the condition, then renormalize the remaining probabilities by dividing by their total. Conditioning on \(P = 3\) keeps the small-business row. Its total is the marginal probability \(\Pr(P = 3)\). Dividing each joint probability in that row by \(\Pr(P = 3)\) gives the conditional distribution in Table 10.6.

Together, the probabilities 0.742 and 0.258 form the conditional distribution of \(D\) given \(P = 3\). Because this probability model uses the historical relative frequencies, they match the conditional proportions computed from counts in Section 10.1; Section 7.4 rounded the default probability to 0.25 for its introductory example.

Among the three purposes, small-business loans have the largest default probability: \(\Pr(D = 1 \mid P = 3) = 0.258\). This is the relevant comparison when we ask which loan purpose has the greatest default risk.

The reverse conditional answers a different question: among the loans that defaulted, what share were for small businesses?

\[ \Pr(P = 3 \mid D = 1) = \frac{822}{9,670} = 0.085. \]

Small-business loans account for only 0.055 of all loans. Even with the largest default risk, they make up only 0.085 of the defaulted loans.

Most defaulted loans are debt-consolidation loans:

\[ \Pr(P = 1 \mid D = 1) = \frac{8,131}{9,670} = 0.841. \]

Debt-consolidation loans have a lower default probability, \(\Pr(D = 1 \mid P = 1) = 0.165\), but they account for 0.845 of all loans. Their large base rate explains why they also account for most defaults.

Exercise 10.2: Complete a renewal model

A subscription company uses the following joint probability model for a randomly selected customer next year. One probability is missing.

Current tier Renews Does not renew Total
Basic 0.42 0.28 0.70
Premium 0.24 ? 0.30
Total ? ? 1.00
  1. Find the missing Premium/Does not renew joint probability.
  2. Complete the marginal distribution of renewal status.
  3. Find the conditional distribution of renewal status for Basic customers.
  4. Find the conditional distribution of renewal status for Premium customers.
  5. Find \(\Pr(\text{Premium}\mid\text{Renews})\).
  6. An analyst says 0.24 is the Premium renewal rate. Explain what 0.24 represents and give the Premium renewal rate.
Solution

The Premium row must sum to its marginal probability of 0.30, so the missing joint probability is

\[ 0.30-0.24=0.06. \]

Adding down the columns gives the marginal renewal distribution:

\[ \Pr(\text{Renews})=0.42+0.24=0.66, \qquad \Pr(\text{Does not renew})=0.28+0.06=0.34. \]

The completed joint probability model is

Current tier Renews Does not renew Total
Basic 0.42 0.28 0.70
Premium 0.24 0.06 0.30
Total 0.66 0.34 1.00

For Basic customers, divide the two joint probabilities in the Basic row by the row’s marginal probability, 0.70:

\[ \Pr(\text{Renews}\mid\text{Basic})=\frac{0.42}{0.70}=0.60, \qquad \Pr(\text{Does not renew}\mid\text{Basic})=\frac{0.28}{0.70}=0.40. \]

For Premium customers, divide by 0.30:

\[ \Pr(\text{Renews}\mid\text{Premium})=\frac{0.24}{0.30}=0.80, \qquad \Pr(\text{Does not renew}\mid\text{Premium})=\frac{0.06}{0.30}=0.20. \]

Among renewing customers, the Premium share is

\[ \Pr(\text{Premium}\mid\text{Renews})=\frac{0.24}{0.66}=0.3636. \]

The value 0.24 is the joint probability that a customer is Premium and renews. The Premium renewal rate conditions on Premium customers, so it is \(0.24/0.30=0.80\).

WarningCommon mistake: \(\Pr(A \mid B)\) and \(\Pr(B \mid A)\) are different numbers

Conditional probabilities are asymmetric. The two share a numerator — the joint probability of \(A\) and \(B\) — but divide it by different base rates (marginal probabilities), so one can be tiny while the other is close to one.

Most tall people aren’t in the NBA, but most people in the NBA are tall: \(\Pr(\text{NBA} \mid \text{tall})\) is minuscule while \(\Pr(\text{tall} \mid \text{NBA})\) is close to one. Mathematically,

\[ \Pr(\text{NBA} \mid \text{tall}) = \frac{\Pr(\text{NBA and tall})}{\Pr(\text{tall})}, \qquad \Pr(\text{tall} \mid \text{NBA}) = \frac{\Pr(\text{NBA and tall})}{\Pr(\text{NBA})}. \]

Adult heights are roughly normal, so about half of adults are taller than average. The first conditional therefore divides a tiny joint probability by about 0.5 and stays tiny. The second divides that same joint probability by the much smaller probability of being in the NBA, producing a conditional probability near 1.
