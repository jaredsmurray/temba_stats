1  Visualizing a Single Quantitative Variable

The first thing we want to do with a quantitative variable is see what values it takes and how often. We’ll start with a concrete example: sale prices of single-family homes in ZIP code 78752 in Austin, Texas.

We’ll look at 134 home sales from a single ZIP code in Austin. We have a column called latest_price recording the most recent sale price in dollars. Our first goal is to visualize the distribution of sale prices to get a sense for typical values, extreme values, and other features of the data. The two visualizations we’ll use most are the histogram and the density plot.

1.1 Histograms

A histogram lets us visualize the distribution of a variable by dividing the range of the data into bins and counting how many observations fall in each bin. The x-axis shows the price range, the y-axis shows the count, and each bar’s height tells you how many homes sold for a price in that bin’s range.

Most homes sold for somewhere between $150,000 and $600,000, with a cluster around $250,000–$450,000. A handful sold for over $800,000, stretching the right side of the distribution out.

1.2 Density plots

A density plot is basically a smoother version of a histogram. Here’s an example, using the same data:

Instead of a “staircase” of bars, the density plot gives us a smooth curve. The basic idea, however, is the same: the bars in a histogram are high when there are many data points in the range of its x-values, while the density curve is high at prices where there are more observed sales close to that price. For example, the density is much higher around $370,000 than $180,000 because there are many more homes with sale prices near $370,000 than $180,000. That is, the data are more dense around $370,000 than around $180,000 (even though no single house in this dataset sold for $370,000).

The construction of a density curve is more involved than a histogram’s; don’t worry about the details.

Definition 1.1: Density curve

A density curve describes how the observed values of a quantitative variable are distributed. The total area under the curve is one, and the area under the curve over any interval is roughly the proportion of observed values in that interval.

The area property is easiest to see in a big dataset, so let’s look at a new example. The ercot data records electricity demand — the load, measured in megawatts — for the North Central region of the Texas power grid, the zone containing Dallas–Fort Worth, over the years 2016 through 2020. ERCOT, the grid’s operator, collected the measurements. We average the hourly measurements within each day, giving us 1,827 daily means. Figure 1.3 shows the density curve of daily mean load with two regions shaded: each one contains about 10% of the observed days, chosen from different parts of the distribution.

The two shaded regions don’t look alike. The left one spans only about 778 MW, because demand is dense there: a narrow band near the center collects about 10% of the days. The upper region stretches across about 3,236 MW to gather the same share from the thinner right tail. The blue curve continues slightly beyond the largest observed daily mean because the density smoother spreads some mass beyond the observed range. The area-equals-proportion property is only approximate, but it is a useful guide to what the y-axis units mean (such as they are).

1.2.1 Comparing density plots and histograms

Histograms are, in a sense, closer to the raw data: each bar is a count you could check by hand, which makes them easy to explain and to trust. Density plots are a little more abstract. In exchange, the density curve makes the overall shape easier to see by smoothing what are often small-sample artifacts like low counts and gaps between observed values. Later in this section we’ll use the two together.

To see how much the bin choice can matter, here is the same 78752 price data binned three different ways.

The goal of the histogram is to “smooth out” gaps and other artifacts of smaller sample sizes while providing a faithful representation of the original data. Ten bins look to be too few (although that’s somewhat subjective). Sixty bins produces a choppy picture that isn’t much better than the rug. The appropriate choice of bins will be context dependent, but any software producing histograms will make use of broadly sensible defaults. It’s a good idea to vary the number of bins and see how the picture changes. Typically you’ll find there are a broad range of choices that produce similar results visually. A density plot swaps the bin choice for a bandwidth — a smoothing parameter that measures how close two prices are — and the bandwidth adjusts the curve in much the same way.

Small bandwidths act like many small bins, and large bandwidths act like a few large bins. The default bandwidth is chosen by some more involved statistical calculations. It’s often quite good, particularly in large samples, but again it’s a good idea to vary it a little and see how the picture changes.

The smoothing in the density plot draws less attention to gaps likely due to small sample sizes, e.g. between the two extreme prices and the rest of the data. But by tracking the data a little more closely, the histogram draws attention to extreme values that might be important to the analysis.

We don’t have to choose. If we rescale a histogram to have a total area of one like a density curve, we can plot them on top of one another. In the rescaled histogram, each bar’s area — not its height alone — represents the proportion of observations in that bin. This also mitigates one other problem with the original histogram: it’s difficult to compare across datasets of different sizes. For example, here are two histograms of prices in two different ZIP codes from the Austin housing data:

Both panels use the same count scale, but comparing their shapes is difficult because 78748 has 1,100 sales while 78722 has only 90. Scaling each histogram to a common area (of one) lets us compare shapes despite the different sample sizes. It also lets us overlay the density curve:

1.3 Percentiles, quantiles, and the cumulative distribution function

One more way to visualize a distribution, though we won’t use it much: the (empirical) cumulative distribution function (CDF). The CDF shows the fraction of observations that fall at or below a given value. For example, suppose 25% of homes sold for $300,000 or less. At $300,000, the CDF is 0.25, or 25%.

Before we look more closely at the CDF, let’s define a few terms.

Definition 1.2: Percentile and quantile

The p-th percentile of a distribution is a value at or below which p% of the observations fall (70% of the observations fall at or below the 70th percentile, for example).

A quantile is the same value with its rank written as a proportion instead of a percentage. The 70th percentile and the 0.7 quantile are the same number, as are the 90th percentile and the 0.9 quantile (and so on).

A percentile isn’t always uniquely defined, especially in small datasets or datasets where we see the same value many times, but we don’t need to worry about that for our purposes. In the hypothetical house-price example above, $300,000 is the 25th percentile of prices in 78752. A more salient example for many of you will be the percentile ranks reported alongside standardized test scores. For example, if you’d scored 655 on the GMAT Focus Edition in 2025 that would beat about 90% of test takers — i.e., 655 is the 90th percentile of GMAT Focus scores over the relevant period (2020-2025 according to GMAC). If you had scored 625 on the same test, that would beat about 79% of test takers, so 625 is the 79th percentile.

Some percentiles/quantiles are used often enough to get their own names — the 50th percentile or 0.50 quantile is known as the median, for example. You may also see references to quartiles, which are the values that split a dataset into four equal parts. The first quartile (Q1) is the 25th percentile (0.25 quantile), the second quartile (Q2) is the median, and the third quartile (Q3) is the 75th percentile (0.75 quantile).

Once you’re familiar with them, plots of CDFs can be a nice companion to our existing visualizations because there’s no need to choose bins or smoothing parameters at all, and it’s easy to read off percentiles (at least approximately) from the plot. Here’s the CDF for our Austin 78752 prices:

1.4 Box and violin plots

A box plot is a compact representation of a distribution that highlights the three quartiles (25th, 50th, and 75th percentiles) and any extreme values. This is a box plot of the home sales in 78752:

The box begins at the 25th percentile and ends at the 75th. The height of the box — the distance between the 75th and 25th percentiles — is the interquartile range (IQR). The line inside the box marks the median (50th percentile). The whiskers reach out to the smallest and largest values within 1.5 times the IQR of the box edges, and any points past the whiskers are drawn individually as potential outliers.

WarningCommon mistake: An “outlier” is not (always) an invalid data point

The 1.5-IQR rule is a convention, not a law. A point identified as an outlier in a box plot might be a perfectly valid observation from a skewed distribution, or it might be a data-entry error (or something else). You should never delete an outlier just because it was flagged by a rule like the 1.5-IQR rule. You should investigate whether it can be corrected and how sensitive your analysis is to the outlying observations.

A violin plot is essentially a density plot mirrored on both sides of an axis, often combined with a box plot in the center. Violins are most useful for comparing distributions across groups, which we’ll do when we compare groups later in the course. For a single variable, they’re a nice way to show both the density shape and the summary statistics.
