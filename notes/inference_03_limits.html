18  The Limits of Statistical Inference

Let’s recap what we’ve found over the last two chapters. If we treat the 81 companies as a random sample from the population of SAP firms — or at least representative enough that they are an “as-if” random sample — then neither Nucleus nor SAP’s claims hold up. The 3.1-point gap Nucleus reported is almost exactly the size of a typical estimation error from a sample of 81 firms, so it is entirely plausible that the true average ROE among all SAP customers is equal to the industry benchmark of 15.7%. Claiming that SAP customers are 20% less profitable than their peers is not supported by the data.

On the other hand, SAP’s advertised 32% advantage would imply a true average ROE of about 20.7%, which sits far outside the confidence interval we computed. Claiming that SAP customers are 32% more profitable than their peers is not supported by the data either.

18.1 Which customers does the sample describe?

The 81 companies were a convenience sample: Nucleus used the publicly traded customers listed on SAP’s website because their identities and financial records were available. SAP did not select them through a random draw from its customer list. Their average ROE may differ systematically from that of other SAP customers, and the confidence interval does not account for that difference.

The data can still inform our judgment about SAP’s advertised advantage. It stands to reason that SAP would showcase relatively successful customers. If these firms have higher ROE than SAP’s other customers, their low average makes the advertised advantage harder to reconcile with the data. This argument depends on how SAP selected the firms; increasing the number sampled from the same source would not establish that they represent all SAP customers.

Exercise 18.1: More responses from the same source

Suppose a retailer surveys customers who volunteer through a loyalty-program email. It increases the number of responses from 200 to 20,000 and reports a much narrower confidence interval for mean satisfaction among all customers.

Explain why the larger sample does not establish that the interval describes all customers. What would you need to investigate about how respondents compare with the customers the retailer wants to describe?

Solution

More responses can reduce sampling variability without removing differences between volunteers and other customers. Investigate whether loyalty members and people who choose to respond have different satisfaction levels from nonmembers and nonrespondents. The narrower interval does not account for those possible differences.

18.2 What uncertainty does an interval measure?

The intervals in these chapters quantify sampling variability under an assumed model for how the data arose. They do not account for biased sampling, missing observations, or errors in how a variable was measured. More observations can reduce the standard error while leaving those problems unchanged.

For SAP, the interval shows that 81 randomly sampled firms could leave considerable uncertainty about mean profitability. Choosing customers from the website may introduce a systematic difference that this interval does not measure. But if we accept the argument that SAP showcases firms with relatively high ROE, we still have qualitative evidence against its advertised 32% advantage: the likely selection bias would make the sample look more profitable than SAP’s customers as a whole.

Exercise 18.2: What does the interval cover?

Suppose a service center randomly samples completed calls and reports an approximate 95% confidence interval of 4–6 minutes for mean recorded waiting time. Its records omit callers who hang up before receiving service, and the timer starts only after a caller finishes an automated menu.

Assume the interval appropriately accounts for random sampling of the recorded completed calls. What uncertainty does it quantify? Explain why it does not establish that the mean total wait for all callers lies between 4 and 6 minutes.

Solution

The interval quantifies sampling uncertainty about the mean recorded wait among completed calls. It omits both the callers who abandon the queue and the time spent in the automated menu. Random sampling of the available records does not make those records describe every caller’s full wait.
