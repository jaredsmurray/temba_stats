6  Introduction to Probability and Sampling

Up until now, we’ve been analyzing data we already have by computing or summarizing the distributions of its variables. In most of these examples we really want to know about data we don’t have: the sale price of a new house, the economic of all U.S. adults (not just the ones we interviewed), load on the electrical grid tomorrow. The statistics and visualization tools we’ve seen describe data we have; we use probability to describe data we don’t have, either because we didn’t measure it or because the interesting events haven’t happened yet. In either case, we can use probability to reason about what we expect to see in the future, how likely different outcomes are, and what decisions we should make in the face of that uncertainty.

Let’s look at an example. Suppose you’re considering funding one of two LendingClub loans at the end of 2012 (where our data stop). Both loans are for the same amount. The first is a small-business loan and the second is a debt-consolidation loan. The two applications are otherwise identical (in terms, borrower credit history, etc.) and if paid in full you will get the same return on your investment. Which will you pick? Splitting the historical LendingClub loans by purpose gives us the default rate for each of these two kinds:

Which loan would you fund, and what’s the chance it would default?

Without any additional information you’d probably pick the debt-consolidation loan and say there’s a 16.5% chance of default. You did a number of things all at once: you made the (at least partially) subjective decision that the historical data were representative of future outcomes, you constructed a rule for assigning probabilities to uncertain outcomes (a probability model), estimated its parameters (the two default probabilities), and used that model to make a decision. In the next few chapters we’re going to make this process a bit more formal so we can continue to build on it.

6.1 What is probability?

We’ve made reference to probability several times above, but we haven’t defined it yet. What is probability? When we say there’s an 80% chance that the Texas Longhorns football team will win their game against Oklahoma, we’re making a probability statement: Numerically quantifying the chance that an uncertain event will take place. There are different ways we can think about that number. We could say that if we could play the game over and over and over, about 80% of the time (or four times out of five) Texas would win. Or we could say something like: Based on what I know about the game and the two teams, I think it’s four times more likely that Texas will win than it is they will lose (or tie).

Both are valid interpretations of the same probability statement, but they come from different perspectives. The first is a long-run frequency interpretation: If we could repeat the game many times, the Longhorns would win about 80% of the time. A more natural long-run frequency interpretation would be something like: among all historical games where the two teams were similar to the Texas and Oklahoma teams this year, the Texas-like team won about 80% of the time, so there’s an 80% chance this Texas team will win the game this year.

The second is a subjective interpretation: It represents my personal belief about the outcome of a single game. I might have arrived at that belief by looking at historical data, but it is still a personal assessment of the chance of a single event. A subjective probability statement is often expressed in terms of betting odds: I would take a bet on Texas that returns my $4 stake plus at least $1 in profit if Texas wins, and I would not take a bet that paid less than that. That 80% chance is just another way of expressing the same idea: I think it’s four times more likely that Texas will win than it is they will lose (or tie).

6.1.1 A simpler example

Let’s consider an even simpler example: We’re going to make a bet on a single coin toss. If the coin comes up heads I pay you a dollar. If it comes up tails you pay me two. Would you play this game with me? Maybe once, for fun, but probbaly not if the rules are I pay you $1,000 if you win and you pay me $2,000 if I do. You’re assuming – at least I’m assuming that you’re assuming – there’s an equal chance that the coin comes up heads or tails when we play our game. That’s a subjective probability assessment about the outcome of a one-off event.

What’s the long-run frequency interpretation? Imagine playing this game over, and over, and over. Now nobody wants to play the low-stakes $1/$2 version of the game; you might win a round or two and there’s even a chance you’ll be up after a few rounds. But play long enough and – if the coin is fair – we’ll each have one close to half the bets, but you paid me twice what I paid you. The only fair game is one where our payouts are equal - the longer we play the closer to even we’ll tend to be.

6.1.2 A real example: Forecasting the federal funds rate

The Federal Reserve periodically sets a quarter-point-wide target range for its key interest rate (the federal funds rate). The fed funds rate is the interest rate that banks charge each other on overnight loans to shore up reserve balances. It’s a key factor driving borrowing costs for all kinds of debt, including consumer debt like mortgages. Raising the fed funds rate generally leads to broader rate increases, cooling down a hot economy and tamping down inflation. Cutting it has the opposite effect, simulating spending and promoting higher employment rates.

At each scheduled Federal Open Market Committee (FOMC) meeting, the Fed can leave the target unchanged or move it by one or more quarter-point increments. These moves have enormous economic consquences, and there is great interest in forecasting them. The CME FedWatch is a public probabilstic forecast of the target rate based on some modeling simplifications (targets shift at most 0.25%, or 25 basis points at each meeting) and trading on futures markets – essentially bets placed by savvy investors on where they think rates will go. Balancing the money on either side of the wager gives an implied probability statement about how likely different FOMC decisions are. The price of futures contracts represents, essentially, a consensus view of how likely each potential rate decision is, the same way the Vegas money line on the Red River game (that is, the payout for a given wager) represents the consensus view of how likely it is Texas will win. By some natural metrics, it is pretty accurate over short windows in many regimes.

6.2 Probability distributions and probabilty models

A probability distribution is a rule (a mathematical function) that assigns probabilities to all possible outcomes of a random or uncertain process. Probabilities are numbers between 0 and 1 that follow rules we spell out in Section 7.2. Based on our historical data about small-business loans, we might say there is a 25.8% chance (a 0.258 probability) that a new small-business loan will default, and a 74.2% chance that it won’t. That’s a simple probability distribution. A probability distribution is a mathematical abstraction of the observed data distributions we’ve already seen, and it has its own mean, median, and standard deviation. We define those summaries in Section 8.1.

6.2.1 What is a probability model?

Definition 6.1: Probability model

A probability model is a probability distribution we construct to approximate the outcomes of some real-world process.

We built one at the start of the chapter without calling it that. Table 6.1 gives the historical default rate for each kind of loan; a probability model instead states the chance that a future loan of that kind defaults. Read the small-business row: a new small-business loan defaults with probability 0.258 and is repaid with probability 0.742. That’s the two-outcome distribution from the last section, now written down for both kinds of loan.

For a sufficiently interesting problem a probability model will almost always be wrong in some way. But a good probability model is useful if it captures the key features of the real-world process we’re trying to understand. While you can write down a complete probability model mathematically or algorithmically without ever touching any data, typically the model will at least have some parameters that we estimate from data (e.g. by plugging historical default rates into our probability model as we just did). A parameter is just a feature of the probability distribution, and many of these are familiar to us from our work in earlier chapters — the mean, the variance, the standard deviation. We defined these in terms of observed data, but each has an analogue for a probability distribution that describes unobserved data.

6.3 What is sampling, what is a sample, and what is a representative sample?

A sample is simply a collection of observations. Samples can be generated (broadly) in two different ways:

Finite-population sampling takes from some broader overall population. The Federal Reserve’s household survey SHED asks a randomly selected sample of adults about their finances and perceptions of the overall economy. We saw its economic-conditions ratings in the chapter on categorical variables. The goal is to learn about the whole population, not just the people who happen to show up in the sample. The 2025 survey found, for example, that 24.5% of the people in the sample rated economic conditions as “Good”.

Probability-model sampling generates repeated realizations of a random process, as when we flip a coin repeatedly, roll a die, or play Plinko. The board in Figure 6.1 stays fixed, but each chip can take a different path. Repeating the drop produces a sample of landing slots.

Not all samples are random. Before we continue, it’s important to remember that not all samples are random. When you’re in class, you and your classmates comprise a sample of all adults currently in the United States. But you’re not a randomly selected sample, and as a cohort you differ from that overall population in important ways. If we tried to use you as “data” to learn about academic achievement in the overall population our estimate would be wildly inaccurate. We perfer random samples in the finite population setting because they are a strong hedge against this kind of bias – most random samples are representative of the population from which they are drawn.

The way we use a random sample doesn’t depend on the way we got it (usually). For the kinds of analyses we’ll do, whether a sample is from a finite population we want to learn about or a probability model we want to estimate – like the model for future loan defaults – won’t matter. In the economic conditions survey, close to 25% of U.S. adults rated the economy as “good”. Let’s imagine that in the true, complete population, exactly 25% of adults would rate the economy “Good” if asked. If we select one of them completely at random, so everyone has the same chance of getting picked, what is the chance that person has a “Good” rating? Twenty-five percent.

Now imagine you roll a fair four sided die. What’s the chance you get a four? Twenty five percent again. The proportion of 4’s in 13,000 of these rolls looks just the proportion of respondents rating the economy “Good” when we sample them completely at random.

Now imagine we didn’t know whether the die was fair, and all we saw were the outcomes of the 13,000 rolls (whether we got a four or not). We could use that to learn about whether the die was fair: If only ten percent of the rolls were fours, we’d be very, very suspicious that the true chance of rolling a 4 was less than 25%, and probably closer to 10%. If 25.000001% of the rolls were fours, we’d probably be willing to chalk that up to chance.

Similarly, we don’t know the true proportion of adults in the U.S. who rate the economy “Good”, but we do know the opinions of 13,000 randomly selected adults. It’s close to 25%, which gives us pretty strong evidence that whatever the right answer is, it’s probably also close to 25%.

The next section of these notes formalizes this idea – how do we make inferences about a population or random process when we only have access to samples from them? For now, just know that we can move back and forth between these two notions of sampling whenever we want. It can be convenient to treat the 13,000 respondents to the survey as 13,000 dice rolls when we model them.

For now, there are two things to remember:

  1. We can use a sample from a larger population to learn about the population, and we can use a sample drawn from a random process — i.e., the observed outcomes of a random or unpredictable process like the one that determines the sale price of a house — to learn about the probability distribution that produces them, and to assess the relative likelihood of future random outcomes.
  2. In either case the sample must represent the population or probability distribution we’re trying to learn about:
    1. If we’re using a sample to learn about a population, the sample cannot have systematic biases. We don’t measure how the country rates economic conditions by standing outside Nordstrom’s and asking people what they think about the economy, because we’ll have systematically more affluent shoppers in our sample than in the population.
    2. If our sample is historical data used to build a probability model, two things must hold. First, the model must be able to describe the historical data: some choice of its parameters should reproduce the important patterns we observed. Second, the historical data must represent the future outcomes we want the model to describe. If important conditions change over time — for example, if new tariffs alter small-business loan default rates — then the historical data may no longer produce useful probabilities for future loans, unless we can also model the effects of these changes.
