6  Introduction to Probability and Sampling

Probability is the mathematical language of uncertainty. It lets us reason coherently about the relative chances of different outcomes and make good decisions with incomplete information. When a survey asks a few hundred or a few thousand Americans how they rate the economy, how accurately can we expect the results to reflect the opinions of the 260+ million adults in the country? When a manufacturing plant produces thousands of widgets daily, how can managers predict tomorrow’s defect rate? What’s the chance the Federal Reserve will keep rates steady at their next meeting? We can make these questions precise — and answer some of them — using probability.

Up until now, we’ve been summarizing and analyzing data we already have. But in essentially every example we’ve seen, we really want to know about data we don’t have: the economic-conditions ratings of all U.S. adults, the sale price of a new house, the Fed’s target range after the next FOMC meeting. One way to think about probability is as describing data you don’t have access to, either because it doesn’t exist yet (the target range after the next FOMC meeting) or because you can’t realistically observe it (the opinions of all U.S. adults). In either case, we can use probability to reason about what we expect to see in the future, how likely different outcomes are, and what decisions we should make in the face of that uncertainty.

Let’s look at an example. Suppose you’re considering funding one of two LendingClub loans at the end of 2012 (where our data stop). Both loans are for the same amount. The first is a small-business loan and the second is a debt-consolidation loan. The two applications are otherwise identical (in terms, borrower credit history, etc.) and if paid in full you will get the same return on your investment. Which will you pick? Splitting the historical LendingClub loans by purpose gives us the default rate for each of these two kinds:

Which loan would you fund, and what’s the chance it would default?

Without any additional information you’d probably pick the debt-consolidation loan and say there’s a 16.5% chance of default. You did a number of things all at once: you made the (at least partially) subjective decision that the historical data were representative of future outcomes, you constructed a rule for assigning probabilities to uncertain outcomes (a probability model), estimated its parameters (the two default probabilities), and used that model to make a decision. In the next few chapters we’re going to make this process a little more formal and introduce some more sophisticated models.

6.1 What is probability?

We’ve made reference to probability several times above (and occasionally in earlier sections), but we haven’t defined it yet. When we say there’s an 80% chance that the Texas Longhorns football team will win their game against Oklahoma, we’re making a probability statement: Numerically quantifying the chance that an uncertain event will take place. There are different ways we can think about that number. We could say that if we could play the game over and over and over, about 80% of the time (or four times out of five) Texas would win. Or we could say something like: Based on what I know about the game and the two teams, I think it’s four times more likely that Texas will win than it is they will lose (or tie).

Both are valid interpretations of the same probability statement, but they come from different perspectives. The first is a long-run frequency interpretation: If we could repeat the game many times, the Longhorns would win about 80% of the time. A more natural long-run frequency that doesn’t invoke an abstract thought experiment would be something like: among all historical games where the two teams were similar to the Texas and Oklahoma teams this year, the Texas-like team won about 80% of the time, so there’s an 80% chance this Texas team will win the game this year.

The second is a subjective interpretation: It represents my personal belief about the outcome of a single game. I might have arrived at that belief by looking at historical data, but it is still a personal assessment of the chance of a single event. A subjective probability statement is often expressed in terms of betting odds: I would take a bet on Texas that returns my $4 stake plus at least $1 in profit if Texas wins, and I would not take a bet that paid less than that. That 80% chance is just another way of expressing the same idea: I think it’s four times more likely that Texas will win than it is they will lose (or tie).

The Federal Reserve sets a quarter-point-wide target range for its key interest rate. At each scheduled Federal Open Market Committee meeting, it can leave that range unchanged or move it by one or more quarter-point increments. An increase is a hike; a decrease is a cut.

When fair betting markets with more or less rational participants exist, these kinds of subjective assessments, aggregated across many participants, can accurately represent uncertainty about future events. The CME FedWatch tool uses the prices of futures contracts to estimate the probability of different outcomes for successive Federal Reserve meetings. By some natural metrics, it is pretty accurate over short windows in many regimes. The price of futures contracts represents, essentially, a consensus view of how likely each potential rate decision is.

Technical detail 6.1: Where the FedWatch probabilities come from

FedWatch backs its probabilities out of market prices, the way you might back a win probability out of betting odds. Suppose a bet pays $100 if the Fed’s target range ends up at 3.75–4.00%, and people are willing to stake about $25 on it. They are treating that outcome as having a probability of about \(25/100 = 0.25\). A small stake for a big payoff means the market considers the outcome unlikely. Traders make exactly this kind of bet on where the federal funds rate will end up, through contracts called fed funds futures, and the prices they pay imply a probability for each target range. Prices move with the news, so any probabilities you read off are a snapshot.

Here is what those market prices implied on July 9, 2026, when the target range stood at 3.50–3.75% and the next two FOMC meetings were scheduled for July 29 and September 16. Table 6.2 shows the tool’s probabilities for where the target range will sit after each meeting.

The September branch probabilities do not come from the per-meeting totals in the table alone. CME estimates probabilities for each meeting from monthly Fed Funds futures prices, then combines successive meeting outcomes into cumulative paths. The marginal totals alone cannot tell us how September’s chances depend on July’s outcome.

The tree in Figure 6.1 lays out how September’s chances depend on what happens in July. If the July meeting holds rates steady — the market’s 75.9% case — there is a 51.8% chance of a hike at the September meeting. If July brings a hike instead, there is a 51.9% chance of another hike in September. In both cases the chance of a September move is close to 0.5. The two paths differ in where a move would leave the target range. Under the FedWatch model, no path in this snapshot cuts rates or moves the range by more than one quarter-point at either meeting. In reality there often isn’t such a bright line between the long-run frequency and subjective interpretations. Subjective assessments are formed in part based on experience and historical outcomes, and long-run frequency statements often hinge on subjective assessments (e.g. how we choose “similar” prior games).

6.1.1 Probability distributions

In the first few sections of these notes we looked at ways to describe and visualize the distributions of observed variables in our dataset — a density curve, a table of proportions. Probability gives us a way to describe the distribution of unobserved variables. A probability distribution is a rule (a mathematical function) that assigns probabilities to all possible outcomes of a random or uncertain process. Probabilities are numbers between 0 and 1 that follow rules we spell out in Section 7.2. Based on our historical data about small-business loans, we might say there is a 25.8% chance (a 0.258 probability) that a new small-business loan will default, and a 74.2% chance that it won’t. That’s a simple probability distribution. A probability distribution is a mathematical abstraction of the observed data distributions we’ve already seen, and it has its own mean, median, and standard deviation. We define those summaries in Section 8.1.

6.1.2 What is a probability model?

Definition 6.1: Probability model

A probability model is a probability distribution we construct to approximate the outcomes of some real-world process.

We built one at the start of the chapter without calling it that. For a sufficiently interesting problem a probability model will almost always be wrong in some way. But a good probability model is useful if it captures the key features of the real-world process we’re trying to understand. While you can write down a complete probability model mathematically or algorithmically without ever touching any data, typically the model will at least have some parameters that we estimate from data (e.g. by plugging historical default rates into our probability model as we just did). A parameter is just a feature of the probability distribution, and many of these are familiar to us from our work in earlier chapters — the mean, the variance, the standard deviation. We defined these in terms of observed data, but each has an analogue for a probability distribution that describes unobserved data.

6.2 What is sampling, and what is a sample?

We can think about sampling in two different ways: First and most familiar, as taking a sample from some broader overall population. The Federal Reserve’s household survey, the source of the economic-conditions ratings we saw earlier, asks a sample of adults about their finances and the economy. Their answers describe the population at large. Let’s simplify its goal to estimating the proportion of U.S. adults who rate national economic conditions good or excellent. Suppose the surveyors interview a sample of 1,000 adults, and 510 of them rate conditions good or excellent. What have we learned about the overall population? That depends entirely on how the sample is constructed.

Generally speaking, the highest quality surveys use random sampling to both ensure that a sample is highly likely to be representative of the population of interest and to help us quantify how different the sample and population ratings are likely to be. If we choose the sample completely at random from the population (e.g. by putting everyone’s name on a lottery ticket and pulling them out of a bag), then it is unlikely that we’ll get a sample with a disproportionate number of good-or-excellent ratings relative to the population of interest. The use of random sampling also tells us how to make uncertainty statements about how far our sample proportion of 0.51 (51%) is likely to be from the true population share who rate conditions good or excellent.

We can also think about sampling as generating realizations of a random process (a probability distribution). When we flip a coin ten times and record the outcomes, we’re generating a random sample of size \(n=10\) from a probability distribution that assigns some chance to observing heads and some chance to observing tails. If it’s a fair coin each outcome is equally likely. If we didn’t know whether the coin was fair, we could try to answer that question by flipping it many times and recording the outcomes. If we flip a fair coin enough times, the proportion of heads should be close to one-half. Suppose we flip the coin 1,000 times and see 510 heads (a proportion of 0.51, or 51%). We can use the very same probability tools to ask whether this is evidence of an unfair coin, or whether we happened to produce more heads than tails simply by chance. In this case the “population” is the probability distribution of the coin flip, and the “sample” is the 1,000 flips we observed.

To belabor the point, suppose that exactly 50% of adults rate conditions good or excellent. Now imagine randomly sampling a single adult such that any adult is equally likely to be chosen. The chance we’ll get someone who rates conditions good or excellent is 50%. If we sample 1,000 adults from the population, what we’ve done is basically the same thing as flipping a coin 1,000 times and counting the number of heads. Our 1,000 survey responses are, for all intents and purposes, 1,000 coin flips.1 Now if we didn’t know the exact population share who rate conditions good or excellent, or whether the coin was fair, we could use that observed data to estimate the population proportion or the true probability our coin will come up heads on a single flip in exactly the same way.

For now, two things:

  1. We can use a sample from a larger population to learn about the population, and we can use a sample drawn from a probability distribution — i.e., the observed outcomes of a random or unpredictable process like the one that determines the sale price of a house — to learn about that distribution.
  2. In either case the sample must represent the population or distribution we’re trying to learn about:
    1. If we’re using a sample to learn about a population, the sample cannot have systematic biases. We don’t measure how the country rates economic conditions by standing outside Nordstrom’s and asking people what they think about the economy, because we’ll have systematically more affluent shoppers in our sample than in the population.
    2. If our sample is historical data used to build a probability model, two things must hold. First, the model must be able to describe the historical data: some choice of its parameters should reproduce the important patterns we observed. Second, the historical data must represent the future outcomes we want the model to describe. If important conditions change over time — say new tariffs alter small-business default rates — then the historical data may no longer support useful probabilities for future loans, unless we can also model the effects of these changes.
